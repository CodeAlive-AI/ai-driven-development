## MDPE.25 - Renew a Shared Music or Dance Practice

> **Type:** Method pattern
> **Status:** Stable

### MDPE.25:1 - Problem frame

Use this method to understand or change how different parts of a shared music or movement practice affect one another. Organizers may find that improving a class, a contest or its provision impairs another activity they value. An archive can show how classes, awards and use of the shared floor fitted together in an earlier series.

Participants may reproduce increasingly difficult sequences in class yet struggle with unfamiliar partners at the social evening. A contest that attracts experienced performers can occupy the main social floor. A useful, appreciated phrase may fail an inherited entry rule whose purpose the organizers can no longer explain.

The result can be an explanation of what the arrangement made possible, or a feasible way to preserve participation, develop skill and try, recognize and retain variants. The organizers may keep activities separate, choose a narrower arrangement or stop when the conflict cannot be resolved within their means.

**First useful move.** Follow the connection in a representative past or proposed occasion. What did participants want to do, or what should they be able to do? Which conditions helped, impeded or would constrain them? If a change is sought, identify a connection the people responsible can alter.

Use a direct teaching, rehearsal, production or event method when it supplies the needed result. MDPE.20 addresses a forecast of the practice's development.

### MDPE.25:2 - Problem

Some informal practices develop schools, qualifications or competitions. To establish that development for a community, examine its history. To judge what a contest rewards, examine the actual task, criteria and use of the award.

Participants may want an enjoyable evening, teachers a learnable progression and sustainable work, skilled performers challenging partners and recognition, organizers a viable recurring event, and tradition bearers continued access to a meaningful repertoire. One person can share several interests. Examine which the arrangement can serve together.

How do these contributions affect the intended occasion and its continuation? A better-taught course can still occupy the only social floor. A more consistent panel can still reward an achievement that tells the organizer little about social partnering.

### MDPE.25:3 - Forces

An improvement for one activity can have the following consequences for another.

| Useful contribution | What can be impaired | Question that changes the response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A short entry into social participation | Preparation for difficult or unfamiliar situations | What must a newcomer do on this floor, and what can be learned later? |
| A demanding school progression | Time and money for participation; variation that the syllabus does not teach | Which exercises improve the receiving activity, and which serve another legitimate aim? |
| Competition, recognition and visible progress | Partner responsiveness, access across experience groups, willingness to experiment | What is actually rewarded, who can perceive it, and what later opportunities depend on the result? |
| Stable exemplars and stylistic continuity | Useful novelty and recognition of its contributors | Which distinctions must remain, for which use, and who can revise that claim? |
| Shared provision and a common event | Independent development of incompatible activities | What should be shared, what should have a separate setting, and what connecting opportunity is worth maintaining? |

A clearer explanation can leave a teacher with less income, an organizer with more work or a participant without a competitive outlet. If the group still needs a change, consider different provision, an agreed trade-off or rejection of the proposal.

### MDPE.25:4 - Solution

Identify the contributions that sustain the practice, what each supplied or could supply to the shared activity, and the evidence for any effects attributed to it. To design a response, identify the intended participation, the actual or suspected difficulty, the means available and who can change or supply what the proposal requires.

Start with the connection the question concerns. An earlier explanation, timetable, test or supported proposal may answer the question. For a design, use the intended occasion to identify what its parts need to supply. Check that their requirements can be met together, and use evidence of what actually happened for claims about provision, use or continued participation.

#### MDPE.25:4.1 - Start from the participation that should remain possible

Choose an occasion representative of the question. A newcomer at a social evening may want to arrive without a regular partner, find willing partners, enjoy dancing, recover from an unfamiliar response and come back. An ensemble may want to retain its recognizable sound while a new member introduces a variation. For a past occasion use what is known about the participants' aims and experience.

What preparation, access and support does each occasion require? A social evening can welcome newcomers while a demanding competition requires preparation. Preserve both opportunities when both are wanted and feasible.

Identify the time, space, money and capabilities each activity needs. Who controls the relevant timetable, teaching offer, entry rules, access and payment? For a proposed change, check the organizer’s or rule holder’s authority. Teaching expertise can establish a teaching capability, and a prize recognizes the achievement assessed at that event.

An existing arrangement that meets the requirements can be retained. A failed use or an opportunity worth its cost can warrant a change.

#### MDPE.25:4.2 - Trace the connections in a past or proposed occasion

Take an observed episode or state the premises of a constructed one. Trace relevant preparation, selection and support before it, then what followed or could follow under the proposed conditions. Determine which contributions and consequences the evidence connects to the episode and where an explanation remains open.

A learner completes a memorized sequence with the instructor, then interrupts an unfamiliar partner who pauses. Perhaps the instructor supplied an unnoticed cue, the lesson rewarded sequence completion, or the contest encouraged practice of a fixed routine. Compare these explanations with limited practice, an unclear signal, an unsuitable track or a partner choosing another activity. What in the record distinguishes them?

Look for connections that matter to the question. For a proposed repair, identify which can change. For example,

- a classroom result is used as evidence for a different receiving capability;
- a judge sees amplitude but cannot observe the partner response on which the social use depends;
- contest recognition controls teaching invitations, so changing the contest can also change someone's livelihood;
- a long teaching block occupies the only social floor;
- participants learn from a record that omits the condition under which a movement should be varied or stopped;
- a retained label groups music, movement and events, and the project mistakenly treats them as one unchanged subject.

Limit the causal claim to what the evidence supports. An association can justify asking how a school or contest contributed to a change in participation. A study of one city and period can suggest what to investigate in another setting.

Keep rival explanations that change the answer or the proposed repair. A change can be justified under more than one explanation even when the cause remains uncertain.

#### MDPE.25:4.3 - Construct an arrangement by working backward from its receiving use

Working backward from the occasion, identify what each activity needs and what existing work, participant or service can supply it. Develop a new contribution where it is needed to remedy a missing contribution, resolve incompatible requirements or realize a worthwhile opportunity.

If partners need to notice a pause and respond to it, an instructor can supplement or replace a continuous sequence drill with practice in noticing the cue, choosing a response and recovering when the expected continuation fails. Use the relevant musical and spatial conditions or explain what the simplified exercise prepares. To claim transfer, examine performance under the changed conditions. Arrange a qualified instructor when one is needed.

If the award selects an achievement different from the one needed, compare changes to the task, observation, consequences or relation between events. Possible responses include the following.

1. Retain a stage contest for its own audience, but stop using its result as a qualification for social partnering or teaching an unrelated capability.
2. Use an improvisational task with unfamiliar partners and music when the sought result is responsive partnering. Explain what observers can assess and what still needs the partner's account.
3. Add a separate opportunity for experiments, with its own stated conditions, so an unfamiliar variant need not first win under criteria designed for the established repertoire.
4. Keep distinct event or teaching branches where their desired results cannot be met in one setting, and deliberately preserve a useful connection between them.

The organizers can combine responses. Random pairing changes how partners are assigned. For enjoyment, examine participants’ accounts. Assess access or evaluation bias from evidence about the corresponding rules and their operation.

When continuity matters, identify the feature to preserve and the allowed variation. Musical phrasing, a partner relation, attribution, repertoire and the social occasion can call for different choices. [MDPE.Application:5.3](52-mdpe-application-5---participation-learning-recognition-and.md#mdpeapplication53---connect-style-method-structure-and-acquisition) relates those distinctions to the Method's organization and the performer's skill.

#### MDPE.25:4.4 - Compare whole arrangements, including who bears the change

Follow each serious proposal through the occasion. Include the current arrangement when retaining it is possible, and use the same requirements for each comparison.

Count each real use of space and instructor time, including simultaneous demands and the work of making the resources available. Count a shared contribution once when it serves both activities. A second room needs the floor, sound, staff and affordable access the activity requires. Moving advanced practice to another day can exclude people unable to attend twice. Welcoming newcomers can create a paid or unpaid demand on skilled participants.

Set aside proposals that would defeat a binding requirement. Compare the feasible alternatives on relevant differences in effort, access, achievement, continuity and development. Keep distinct criteria visible when combining them would hide a loss or a veto.

A technically suitable proposal can still lack a provider or a participant's agreement. If an agreement is needed, negotiate the work, compensation or allocation with whoever can decide. An existing agreement may suffice. If provision remains unavailable, consider a smaller feasible arrangement or separate activities. If provision is the question, identify what is missing and what that prevents.

#### MDPE.25:4.5 - Arrange acquisition and provision at the point where they are needed

A participant may need an explanation, an opportunity to learn, access to someone already capable, or a change to the task or environment. Identify the missing contribution and check the result appropriate to it.

A newcomer may need the pause-and-response relation explained, practice that makes the cue perceptible, or a willing partner. An experienced performer may already know the response but miss a quiet cue in a noisy room. Reducing noise can make that skill usable there.

When learning is wanted, establish what the performer can do, which help was available and used, and the conditions of the intended performance. Keep useful support that belongs in the receiving activity. Change or withdraw it when the task requires independence, the support will be unavailable, or another arrangement better meets the requirement. Use suitable evidence of performance under the conditions claimed. Arrange another attempt if the missing result warrants it.

Arranging a class and providing a place to use the skill solve different problems. A party can supply the occasion while an instructional or performing capability is still missing. C.36.RP distinguishes preserving an answer, making a Method obtainable and keeping a later occasion in which participants can use it.

#### MDPE.25:4.6 - Carry out the change and follow its consequences through receiving use

A request for a proposal can finish by stating what is feasible and any conditions still unresolved. For implementation, use the agreed resources to carry out the teaching, event, selection or support work. For a historical question, examine the provision, activity or result the surviving evidence establishes.

Use an observation suited to the claim, including an earlier result obtained under relevant conditions. A successful demonstration with the instructor's continuous cue establishes an assisted classroom performance. For an unassisted evening with unfamiliar partners, look for evidence of that performance. Several changes can affect participation, so assess other plausible explanations before attributing a decline to an intervention.

For a proposed change, compare the expected gain and other possible consequences. For a completed change, establish what happened. A separate advanced room may keep experienced participants at the event while reducing their time on the shared floor. A new award can change what performers practice to win. A shorter class can increase social time while omitting a necessary entry skill.

If further repair is wanted, return to the connection affected. An unavailable room changes the timetable; it need not alter how an audible cue worked in a different room. Keep the conclusions whose conditions still hold.

#### MDPE.25:4.7 - Renew the criteria as well as the selected repertoire

Keep a way for practitioners to bring forward an otherwise excluded variant. When one challenges a rule, identify the intended use it supports and the condition it violates. Compare these possibilities where they bear on the question.

- the variant fails a still-necessary condition;
- the condition described the predecessor too narrowly and should change;
- the variant serves a different valuable use and should remain a separate branch.

Try the receiving use when the available evidence leaves an important difference unresolved and a feasible trial could resolve it at a justified cost. A spectator, partner and teacher may observe different parts of the response. If their accounts must be combined, explain how the decision uses each observation and handles disagreement.

When revising a canon, retain the historical account of the repertoire, its contributors and the achievements the old criteria recognized. Identify the event or practice covered by the new rule. Wider adoption requires evidence at that scope.

Finish when the finding, proposal, decision or realized arrangement answers the question. Reconsider the affected part when a failed use, lost resource, changed group or previously excluded variant challenges a premise being used.

#### MDPE.25:4.8 - What must hold together

For a proposal, connect the account of the problem to a feasible arrangement. For each needed contribution, show how it can be supplied. Teaching may prepare a performer, permitted assistance may support the performance itself, and access may make an existing provider or resource available. Check competing demands on people, time and space. For an implemented change, examine what the receiving activity showed. A failed trial can identify a limit, justify stopping or prompt further development of the affected part.

When the request concerns an arrangement or proposal, identify which contributions can be obtained. If a necessary connection is missing, identify the limit and any useful narrower option. An account of past work identifies what was proposed, carried out, used or achieved, to the extent the evidence supports each claim.

Preserve the purpose, constraints and meaning of the results when varying schedules or providers. Reconsider an inadequate criterion. Moving from social participation to selecting a stage spectacle changes what the comparison should establish.

Show what each contribution must supply, whether it is obtainable and what follows for the occasion. A qualified provider can supply instruction or administrative work without disclosing every internal step. Finish with an adequate answer, the limit imposed by an unavailable contribution or an explicit change of purpose.

### MDPE.25:5 - Archetypal Grounding

#### MDPE.25:5.1 - A complete constructed case: the evening that became a qualification course

This constructed example stipulates the people, resources and observations used to compare arrangements.

Mara organizes a four-hour Friday event. Sol teaches the introductory class, Jules is available to teach the advanced class, and Ren administers its small contest. The same main floor must accommodate a 90-minute class, 90 minutes of contest activity including changes between rounds, and 60 minutes of open social participation. Advanced performers value the contest and the school gains students from it. Newcomers say they have paid for an evening but spend most of it watching or taking instruction.

Mara wants at least 150 minutes on the shared social floor and a feasible challenge for experienced performers. A side studio is available for the first 90 minutes within the budget for rooms and instructors. Before selecting the trial, she confirms adequate sound and space for the advanced class.

Mara first suggests a longer beginner course. Following one newcomer, she finds that the person can already perform the entry exercise. The main floor is unavailable for much of the evening, and the familiar partner supplies cues in class that are missing later. A longer course on the main floor would take more time from social dancing.

The group compares three arrangements. The current schedule preserves the contest and misses the social-time promise. Cancelling the contest frees time and removes a valued challenge and source of recognition. For the selected trial, the main floor has a 30-minute optional clinic, a 60-minute contest and 150 minutes of open social dancing, or 240 minutes in all. The staffed 90-minute advanced class runs in the side studio alongside the clinic and contest. Participants choosing the contest cannot attend the entire advanced class. The announcement states these choices.

A second connection still fails. The contest originally rewards a fixed prepared sequence, while the event promise concerns responsive partnering. Ren explains that this trial evening replaces the fixed-sequence contest with an optional improvisational challenge. The earlier contest results retain their meaning for prepared performance; running that contest on another occasion would require its own timetable and resources. The trial initially retains an inherited entry rule: a submitted variation must include a large visible change, which the organizers expect to make judging possible. Within that entry condition, the task asks performers to sustain a phrase, respond to a partner's pause and resume together with music selected for the event. The observer watches timing and recovery; the partner reports whether the response permitted comfortable continuation. For this trial task, a successful response requires both a timely resumption observed in the phrase and the partner's report of comfortable continuation. A disagreement leaves that episode unresolved and may warrant closer observation if that observation could settle a remaining decision at a justified cost. The challenge recognizes this bounded achievement without ranking overall mastery or granting a general licence to teach.

Sol demonstrates a short phrase with a visible pause, asks the learner to identify the cue and varies its timing. The learner tries a counted cue, then a partner's cue in the phrase. A count can remain where the event permits one. If the claim is performance without it, use a representative unassisted attempt. If a difficulty needs attention, examine the relevant cue, timing, explanation or surroundings. Change the exercise when the evidence supports doing so.

Willing experienced participants agree to a ten-minute welcoming interaction at the start of open dancing. Others can pursue their own activities, and each participant can accept or decline a partner.

Suppose the advertised 150-minute open period takes place and more newcomers remain to the end than at the earlier event. Some experienced participants stay with their familiar group, and newcomers report little contact with them. Several learners resume after a pause with the clinic partner but fail to resume with a different partner. The first observation establishes the timetable result. The others describe that evening and identify possible problems with access and transfer. Attributing the increase in newcomers staying to the end to the trial or claiming a general capability would require further grounds.

The group keeps the open period. Sol has the learners practise the response with a changed cue and partner before adding sequences. Mara and the willing hosts divide their agreed ten minutes into two announced five-minute opportunities, including one after groups have settled. They consider the extra interruption. Ren retains the contest result for its stated task.

Now a participant introduces a very small pause-and-response variant. From the back of the room it looks unimpressive, but partners report that it improves their ability to rejoin after an unexpected pause. The inherited entry rule excludes the variant because its visible change is too small. The group asks what that criterion was for. For a stage spectacle it can remain useful. For responsive social partnering it excludes part of the promised result. Ren removes the local challenge's large-change entry condition and changes the observation position so smaller responses can be seen. He retains the two-part recovery criterion, preserves what the earlier stage-display result established, and keeps both examples with their uses and contributors. The changed criterion itself becomes open to later contrary examples.

Now suppose the side studio becomes unavailable. The advanced class cannot occupy the main floor at the same time as the clinic and contest there. The group compares a separately funded occasion, an alternating programme and a smaller single-room event. If another occasion is unaffordable, it can alternate social and competition evenings with distinct promises and prices, keeping a small clinic where it fits. Participants can no longer attend the two offerings on the same evening. The clinic task and the distinction between contest achievement and social skill still apply.

In this case the group changed the timetable, instruction, award, provision and entry criterion to serve the social evening. Restoring floor time solved one problem. The other changes addressed learners’ use of cues, opportunities to meet partners and the meaning of the award. The trial showed which activities and responses occurred together.

#### MDPE.25:5.2 - A constructed music case: solo achievement and shared ensemble entry

A community percussion group wants newcomers to join an open ensemble session. Its monthly solo challenge rewards a fast, uninterrupted pattern, and the winners are invited to lead the next session. The group can already supply instruments and a quiet room. Its experienced facilitator can teach the cue task and has agreed to lead the current session. Some newcomers can reproduce the pattern alone, yet lose their place when another player changes the cue. A faster solo test would still leave their response to another player’s cue unexamined.

The group keeps the solo challenge for its stated achievement and compares two ways to lead the open session. One continues to appoint its winner automatically. The other uses a short ensemble task: a leader establishes a common pulse, gives an agreed audible entry cue, leaves space for another player, and resumes after that player returns the cue. Use suitable existing evidence or examine an ensemble attempt. The facilitator can observe the cue and resumption. The other players can report whether they could follow it and continue. If the winner’s leadership remains unestablished, the experienced facilitator who has agreed to lead can provide the current session.

For learners who lose the cue, the facilitator uses an easy pattern with a spoken count, then varies the player who gives the audible cue while keeping the pulse and pattern familiar. A count can remain if the open session permits it. Before expanding the pattern, the group checks the skill under the intended conditions. The learners' response, their solo dexterity and the provision of an experienced leader answer different questions.

Suppose a quiet cue that worked in the small room becomes inaudible at a larger event. The organizer can retain the smaller session, arrange a visible cue with the players or obtain adequate sound. Compare the feasibility and consequences of each. The small-room performance and the solo challenge retain their meaning. These are stipulated conditions for choosing what to do at the larger event.

### MDPE.25:6 - Bias-Annotation

The first case gives priority to access to social dancing. A stage ensemble may value a longer demanding rehearsal. Use suitable evidence of what matters to participants and ask them when further clarification is needed before treating a shorter class, mixed-experience interaction or separate competitions as improvements. Experienced participants can prefer familiar partners and decline teaching or welcoming work.

An award can draw attention to the winner while a teacher, partner, organizer or space provider bears the cost of the event. Find out whose participation or work a proposal needs and who controls each relevant decision. When a new variant warrants a local rule change, keep the earlier contributors' names and achievements in the account.

### MDPE.25:7 - Conformance Checklist

For a proposal, check how the parts fit, who is affected, which resources are available and what a changed condition would affect. For a past or implemented arrangement, examine the evidence for the particular finding. Classroom success, available floor time, satisfaction, membership over time and cultural development depend on different observations.

For a decision, explain why the chosen activities fit together and which contributions the choice relies on and any relevant limit on their availability. A historical account can finish with what happened and what remains uncertain. If an observed complication warrants further work, identify what it changes.

Use the questions that bear on the work being assessed.

- What participation was wanted or is planned? Which connection matters to it, and is there a lost opportunity or useful improvement to address?
- Do the proposed contributions fit together in time, space and cost, with capable providers, authority to make the changes and agreement to the contributions being relied on?
- What does the learning task prepare, or what does continuing support let the participant do? What does the performance evidence establish, and what achievement does the award recognize?
- What supports each claim about feasibility, performed change, capability or longer continuation, and which claims remain unresolved?
- Which evidence or change in circumstances warrants reconsidering a criterion or activity? Which independent results still hold?

### MDPE.25:8 - Common Anti-Patterns and How to Avoid Them

**Answering lost participation with more instruction.** A longer course may improve classroom performance while taking time from its use. Follow the participant through the occasion. If a repair is wanted, address the missing access, skill or connection.

**Using one achievement as a general qualification.** A prepared solo or stage performance can leave responsive partnering, leadership and teaching unexamined. Keep the achievement within its task and use suitable evidence, including an earlier demonstration, to assess whether the candidate can do the work the appointment requires.

**Treating experienced participants as free provision.** Before relying on a room or performer, establish the access, equipment, time and agreement the arrangement needs. Compare compensation, a smaller contribution or another feasible arrangement.

**Changing a criterion only to admit a preferred novelty.** Show which receiving use the variant supports and why the current rule excludes it. Retain a necessary condition, revise an inadequate one or keep a separate branch according to that comparison.

**Restarting the entire practice after one premise fails.** Loss of a room can invalidate a timetable without invalidating a useful teaching task or a correctly bounded award. Follow the changed condition to its dependent contribution and retain the others.

### MDPE.25:9 - Consequences

A supported arrangement can let the group retain a valued challenge, make the floor available and change what recognition encourages. Whether people actually participate or alter their practice can then be established at the appropriate scale. A proposal can also identify the missing provider, agreement or resource that prevents implementation.

The design and its coordination take work. Separate activities can require more staffing, access and occasions for people to meet. Shared activities need compatible demands and agreement about who bears the costs. Stop when the answer suffices. Wider adoption, independent reproduction and cultural retention can be investigated on their own terms.

### MDPE.25:10 - Architectural Rationale

Combine activities when they can meet the intended requirements together and the benefits justify their costs. Sharing a room or staff time can save resources. Simultaneous demands can also make the joint event more expensive. Separate activities can protect their purposes while adding staffing, access or ways for people to meet. Compare the feasible arrangements under the local purposes and costs.

Use the intended occasion to discover how the contributions affect one another. Teaching can change what performers can do, awards can change what they practise, and the timetable can make a room available for them to use what they learned. For a design, check how the contributions would fit at that occasion. For a past event, use evidence of the connections and results in question.

Use an adequate teaching or support repair directly when it supplies what is missing. When several changes depend on each other, establish joint feasibility. In Mara's case cancellation is a serious alternative if the staffed rooms cannot support both activities. Her choice depends on her event's purposes, staffing, space and cost.

A group that controls a local selection rule can change it and observe the consequences. That authority does not extend automatically to other events or to a cultural population. Keeping this boundary lets a useful local change finish while a broader historical or continuation question remains open.

### MDPE.25:11 - SoTA-Echoing

When teaching, recognition and provision pull a shared practice toward incompatible aims, compare what each contributes to the intended occasion. One repair may suffice. Other choices include keeping a useful contest, separating activities, reducing the proposed arrangement or dropping it.

Jiménez Sedano's 2019 ethnography describes changes to movement through classroom codification, access through existing salsa networks, the role of contests in professional recognition and the importance of local social occasions for teaching communities. Her 2020 account distinguishes circulation of recorded music from adaptation of movement and relates event labels to their social settings. These historical cases suggest connections to investigate in a new setting. [2019 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.poetic.2019.04.001) and [2020 study](https://doi.org/10.1080/14788810.2019.1696081).

Lisbon Westie Fest's published rules describe random partners, partner changes in preliminary and semi-final rounds, DJ-selected music and teamwork among the criteria. They provide a concrete alternative to a prepared stage display. The effects on participation, longer practice and incentives require evidence of what people did. [Organizer's competition rules](https://lisbonwestiefest.com/competition-rules/).

When responsive partnering is the goal, consider an improvised interaction task or retain an established format that already serves it. Mara's challenge uses timing and a partner's account of comfortable continuation. If they disagree, the result remains open. For the local use, establish whether the existing timetable, access, teaching and award already serve it. Revise those that do not when a change is commissioned.

The studies identify historical connections, the rules specify a contest format and the constructed cases show how to compare locally feasible changes. Claims about which arrangement works better need grounds from the relevant practice. Reconsider a choice when new evidence, an attainable alternative or a changed purpose affects those grounds.
