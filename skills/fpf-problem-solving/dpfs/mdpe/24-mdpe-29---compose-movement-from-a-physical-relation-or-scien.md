## MDPE.29 - Compose Movement from a Physical Relation or Scientific Image

**Type:** Method
**Status:** Stable
**Normativity:** Normative

### MDPE.29:1 - Problem frame

**Use this when** a physical relation or scientific image suggests movement and you need a phrase, variations or a whole composition. A score, demonstration or other recoverable arrangement can specify how the phrases and transitions work and what a partner, performer or viewer needs to perceive for this work.

This technique, developed in Western concert, contemporary and postmodern choreography, generates movement material, varies its energy, time and space, then composes it. Use an existing phrase or another compositional method when it already serves the work.

### MDPE.29:2 - Problem

An attractive pose or gesture can leave the movement between poses, the transition to another phrase, the viewer’s sightline or the ending unspecified. Literal illustration may restrict a composition. Treating a real mechanical limit as a freely variable drawing can produce an infeasible score.

Develop movement that can be repeated or varied where the piece needs it. Identify what the source contributes, including when the chosen artistic operation departs from the physical one.

### MDPE.29:3 - Forces

| Concern | Consequence for construction |
| --- | --- |
| Physical constraint and artistic possibility | A physical premise can rule out a realization; a scientific image can motivate another movement without explaining its mechanism. |
| Recognizable material and consequential variation | Some relation must remain available for the intended comparison, while other features can change enough to develop the piece. |
| Phrase and composition | Locally workable phrases can require an additional transition or a different order to form a whole. |
| Composed score and performance | A specified trajectory shows its timing and joins. A physical model can determine whether the trajectory is feasible under its assumptions. To report what a performer achieved or how an audience responded, use evidence of that realization or reception. |

### MDPE.29:4 - Solution

Recover the relation the source contributes. Use an adequate existing phrase or construct one. Vary it and join phrases where the intended piece requires it. These choices can affect one another. A join that exceeds the available reach may call for a different phrase, and a new ending may change which relation is worth retaining.

#### MDPE.29:4.1 - Make the source useful to a composition

Decide how the source bears on the work. A mechanical relation can rule out a realization under specified conditions. A scientific image can instead suggest a choreographic operation, such as contracting a path in one direction.

Choose a short movement phrase whose start, changes, intervening movement and ending can be recovered. Identify a relation worth retaining, such as an arrival, a partner response, a continuing path or a visible contrast. To create a variant, change a feature that matters to the work. When alternatives are due, compare complete variants, including their endings and any transition the next action needs.

Use MDPE.9 to compare variants when that comparison is needed. For a recommendation or selection, identify which to retain, revise, develop separately or reject and why. Use MDPE.11 when the distinction between prepared material and live interpretation matters. A beginning can establish the chosen relation, a development can transform it and an ending may resolve or suspend it. Bring in movement technique or learning for a specific missing contribution.

#### MDPE.29:4.2 - Investigate the material and compose the whole

Recover the seed well enough to vary it. A path, timing distribution, support, partner relation or quality of transition may be essential. Positions suffice when the performer can also recover the intended motion between them. Choose a demonstration, score, recording or parameterized trace appropriate to the receiving use.

For a mechanical inquiry, derive the effect of the changed quantity under the model. In unpowered free flight under constant gravity, with equal takeoff and landing heights and negligible air resistance, a longer flight requires a greater vertical launch speed and rise. For an artistic use, define the operation you take from the image, such as compressing one spatial dimension while retaining arrival times. Assess that choice for the composition.

For a comparison of phrases, consider each phrase through its ending. For a single phrase, follow the movement through the result requested of it. A different duration can change momentum, emphasis and readiness for the next action. A change of spatial scale can alter visibility or effort. Retain the relation needed for comparison, or identify the new artistic aim that warrants changing it.

Recurrence can make a phrase recognizable, contrast can redirect attention and a changed return can make a transformation perceptible. Determine which participant must be able to perceive which change. A partner may need to feel a transfer through contact. For a visual result, establish the viewer’s position, choosing it when staging permits, and what can be seen from it. Use audience evidence if the question concerns how particular viewers experienced the piece. Join the end of each phrase to the required beginning of the next, accounting for timing, position, support and attention. If the available means rule out a join, compare changes to the join, the phrases, their order or the means.

A usable result can be the selected phrases, their transformations and the complete composition. A score or animation can settle the specified relations. For a question about feasibility, use the relevant mechanical conditions, capability evidence or an appropriate realization. For an observed artistic effect, identify the performance and what supports that effect. An available performer or another realization may already serve the work. Develop a capability when the chosen realization needs it and that development is part of the task.

#### MDPE.29:4.3 - Adapt the construction without losing its distinction

When the seed, the relation to be retained or the conditions of use change, reconsider the choices that depended on them. Recompute affected quantities. In the flight model below, gravity and duration determine the rise. Changing the dancer’s mass alone leaves the modeled rise unchanged. Reconstruct a join when the preceding phrase now ends in a different state. Compare another realization if it can retain the wanted artistic relation.

Use PHY for a physical-model question, SOM for bodily regulation, RHY for rhythmic coordination and HCD for human learning. A suitable earlier calculation, observation or teaching result may answer the question. When further work is needed, an available dance or teaching method may suffice.

### MDPE.29:5 - Archetypal Grounding

The following constructed cases can be inspected as scores or animations. The movement choices and capability conditions are given for the examples. For bodily realization, use familiar movements in an individually suitable range and appropriate instruction. In both scores the performer can remain supported while the hand moves.

#### MDPE.29:5.1 - Case A — Keep an apex in the music without demanding a longer flight

**Situation and physical decision.** A four-second motif rises and returns in the first second, holds low during the second, extends sideways during the third and returns during the fourth. The maker wants a visible summit at 0.5 s followed by a longer low interval. The initial proposal assigns the first second to the unpowered flight of a dancer’s centre of mass under constant gravity, with equal takeoff and landing heights and negligible air resistance.

For a flight of duration T under these assumptions, vertical launch speed is v_y = gT/2 and rise is h = gT²/8. With g = 9.8 m/s² and T = 1 s, the values are 4.9 m/s and 1.225 m. The fictional brief permits only a familiar flight lasting 0.2 s. Its modeled rise is 0.049 m. Requiring five times that flight time would require twenty-five times the rise. These values describe the model.

Compare three candidates:

| Candidate | What it retains | What changes the decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stretch the airborne interval to one second | The proposed flight and midpoint summit | Reject for this brief. The one-second flight lies outside its supplied capability conditions. |
| Place the familiar 0.2-second flight from 0.4 to 0.6 s | The 0.5 s apex and available flight duration | Keep this option if the brief calls for actual flight. It changes the full-second rise-and-return contour. |
| Keep the body supported and give the contour to a small hand path | The full-second contour, midpoint summit and following low interval | Select for the visible contour required here. |

If the brief also requires actual flight, the supported hand path satisfies only its contour requirement. Reconsider timing, available means or the brief.

**Selected phrase.** Take a comfortable supported position without partner contact or required weight transfer. For this score, choose a familiar hand-height change H and sideways reach W. Measure coordinates from the hand’s low home position. The other hand stays at home.

| Time in the four-second motif | Right-hand path |
| --- | --- |
| 0–0.5 s | Rise from (0,0) to (0,H). |
| 0.5–1 s | Descend to (0,0). |
| 1–2 s | Remain at home. |
| 2–3 s | Travel horizontally to (W,0). |
| 3–4 s | Return to home. |

Score each moving segment with a smooth start and finish. For a drawing or animation, interpolate along each segment with q(u) = 3u² − 2u³, 0 ≤ u ≤ 1. This gives zero speed at its endpoints. The hold separates the controlled vertical and horizontal gestures. At four seconds the hand is home. Repeat with the left hand and opposite sideways direction to make the eight-second phrase A.

For phrase B, replace each vertical rise by a diagonal to half the sideways reach at height H and return along it. In a common horizontal frame the mirrored summits are (W/2,H) and (−W/2,H). Keep the durations, each motif’s summit at 0.5 s and the later horizontal out-and-back. The summit height and tempo stay the same. At the same interpolation rate the hand travels farther during the diagonal, so its path speed increases. A summit delayed to one second would lose the midpoint arrival.

**Composition.** The 24-second score gives A, B, then A with both hand-path dimensions halved. The last eight seconds recall the vertical gesture at reduced scale. Each four-second motif ends at home with zero prescribed hand speed, ready for the other hand. The piece ends with the return at 24 s. The viewer sits in front of the dancer with both hands visible. Whether viewers found the change compelling requires evidence of their responses.

**A human practice question.** Suppose a dancer can perform the rise and sideways reach separately but starts the reach before the 1–2 s hold ends. If the task includes learning this coordination, use RHY.12 to retain the complete four-second motif and show its timing, then use HCD.9 for feedback and a further attempt. If bracing interferes instead, SOM.2–.4 can help the dancer and a qualified teacher compare bodily organizations and restore the full phrase under the chosen support. The animation can establish the scored times and joins. A claim about the dancer’s capability needs evidence of performance.

#### MDPE.29:5.2 - Case B — Use relativity to generate a spatial duet, not to explain the dancers' bodies

**Situation and source choice.** The maker wants unfamiliar spatial organization with recognizable phrasing. For a segment aligned with relative motion, proper length L_0 is measured in its rest frame. An observer in another inertial frame measures the positions of its endpoints simultaneously in that frame. The relation is L = L_0 sqrt(1 − v²/c²). Half the proper length requires v/c = sqrt(3)/2, about 0.866. Moving a spectator to another seat changes the view of a figure. That change of viewpoint does not perform the simultaneous endpoint measurement in this calculation.

For the duet, halve one spatial dimension, preserve the other and retain arrival times. That choreographic operation takes an idea from the calculation. Ordinary changes in a dancer’s reach have their own mechanics.

**Generate the phrase.** Work first with a hand trace in a plane in front of a comfortably supported performer. Choose familiar half-width W and height H. All coordinates below are scaled to that person's chosen range. Make an eight-second closed phrase S through five positions:

| Time | Position (x,y) |
| --- | --- |
| 0 s | (−W,0), left-low |
| 2 s | (+W,0), right-low |
| 4 s | (+W,H), right-high |
| 6 s | (−W,H), left-high |
| 8 s | (−W,0), left-low |

Use q(u) = 3u² − 2u³ on each two-second segment. The hand follows a rectangle, reaches zero prescribed speed at each corner and returns to its initial position at eight seconds.

To construct C, replace every x coordinate with x/2 while retaining y and all five arrival times. The width changes from 2W to W. With the same interpolation, horizontal speed and acceleration halve, and the vertical segments keep their timing and extent. The path remains closed and visits the corners in the same order.

Compare C with U, which halves x and y, and F, which performs S in four seconds. Only C preserves both the intended height and eight-second phrase while halving the width. U and F offer other material when height or duration can change. At unchanged reach, F requires a faster path.

**Compose the duet.** Place two performers in separate non-overlapping hand-reach areas, visible side by side from the front. They share eight-second beginnings but do not touch or exchange weight. Use this thirty-two-second fixed score:

| Interval | Performer A | Performer B | Compositional consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–8 s | S | S | Establish the common rectangle and arrival times. |
| 8–16 s | S | S-to-C transition phrase | One width changes while height and shared arrivals persist. |
| 16–24 s | S | C | Sustain the difference so it can be compared. |
| 24–32 s | S-to-C transition phrase | C | Converge on the compressed figure and finish at its common normalized left-low point. |

The transition starts at (−W,0), reaches (+W/2,0) at two seconds, then (+W/2,H), (−W/2,H) and (−W/2,0) at successive two-second arrivals. Its first horizontal segment connects the former left endpoint to the reduced right endpoint. Each segment ends with zero prescribed speed. At 16 s, B’s transition ends at the start of C. At 24 s, B finishes C and can start C again. At 32 s, A’s transition and B’s C end at the same normalized left-low point within their respective ranges.

MDPE.11 distinguishes this fixed arrangement from live interpretation. The performers may choose familiar ranges before beginning. The path order and arrival times are given. With independently chosen ranges the width comparison is within each performer’s own phrases. If equal absolute widths matter, specify compatible ranges and a common spatial reference.

**A practice and observation question.** An animation can expose an incorrect join before anyone performs the duet. Suppose a dancer performing C reaches the corners early and waits. The endpoints match, but the two-second moving intervals do not. For practice, RHY.3 can support a continuing path and RHY.10 can supply shared landmarks. HCD.6 can retain width and duration in one eight-second task, and HCD.9 can give feedback on early arrival. For difficulty changing reach without bracing, use a suitable SOM comparison and return to the same phrase. Retain music or a teacher’s count when it belongs to the intended performance. If independent timing is required, examine what the dancer can do without that support.

The two phrases, their transition and the complete duet score answer the composing question. A front-view plot of the score can establish its designed geometry. A recording of an execution can show the path visible from that position. A performer’s report can address ease or attention, and audience responses can support a claim about the perceived effect. Use those sources where the question concerns the corresponding performance or reception.

### MDPE.29:6 - Bias-Annotation

The source tradition emphasizes deliberate phrase generation and manipulation. The hand paths let a reader inspect geometry and joins in a score or animation before anyone performs them.

### MDPE.29:7 - Conformance Checklist

Check the relations needed for the intended result.

- The physical source constrains a realization or motivates an artistic operation, and the chosen role is explained accurately.
- The seed and its consequential changes can be recovered, including transitions within the phrase.
- When the work calls for comparing variations, the comparison shows what each contributes to the intended whole and why their common relation should stay or change.
- Phrase joins, viewer placement and the ending are specified wherever they affect the result.
- Changed timing, scale, support or receiving conditions lead back to the affected reasoning and construction.

A complete score can answer the composing question. Claims about capability, learning or reception need evidence of the corresponding result under the conditions claimed.

### MDPE.29:8 - Common Anti-Patterns and How to Avoid Them

| Invited mistake | Repair |
| --- | --- |
| Stretch a physically constrained action as if changing a drawing's duration | Recompute the relevant physical relation and compare other realizations of the wanted artistic relation. |
| Keep interesting poses while leaving the movement between them unspecified | Construct the transitions and compare the complete phrase. |
| Let a scientific image claim to explain the body's actual mechanism | State the artistic transformation taken from the image and use an applicable physical account for realization. |
| Require every later variation to illustrate the initial source literally | Follow the developing composition, making a changed artistic aim and its consequences explicit. |

### MDPE.29:9 - Consequences

The maker obtains material that can be varied and connected, with reasons for the chosen composition. When a physical premise changes, examine the realization that depended on it. Another movement may preserve the artistic relation. Choosing among workable results still requires artistic judgement.

### MDPE.29:10 - Architectural Rationale

The source suggests a movement or constrains it. The phrase gives the maker material to vary, and the maker selects and joins variations for the composition. A difficult join can send the maker back to the phrase or its starting idea.

MDPE.9 compares creative alternatives, and MDPE.11 governs the relation between prepared material and live interpretation. The present method supplies the particular construction they can use when a physical relation or scientific image is the material for choreography.
