## KCAE.Reference:2 - Why changing questions defeat fixed representations

An index is a purpose-selected view of source material. Its terms, vectors, extracted entities and summaries retain distinctions that help some operations and remove others. If two different passages obtain the same derived representation, a future question can depend on the difference between them. Searching only that representation cannot recover the difference. This argument concerns a lossy mapping and an unrestricted future question; a lossless representation preserves the distinctions, and a lossy index can settle a particular question if all source situations it treats alike give the same required answer. If the reader must choose one safe action, a common admissible action across all those situations can be enough even when their full sets of actions differ. To give the complete set, recover the differences that change it. If there is no common action for situations the reader still cannot distinguish, obtain the distinguishing information before choosing or report that the action is unsupported.

Keeping the original opens a second route when the decisive distinction can travel from it to the decision outside the view that lost it. Follow that route through the participants who can supply each part within the available time and cost. A catalogue service may find the shelfmark of a closed register. Its custodian may be able to open the volume at that shelfmark, identify the edition containing a clause and disclose that finding to a planner. If the planner is entitled to rely on the report, the three can settle the edition question together. If the commission requires a named reviewer to compare the original wording personally, arrange permitted access for that reviewer. A link behind the failed category filter leaves the material undiscoverable by that route. Even inspection of every source byte can miss the relevant relation.

Questions also change through inquiry. A request for more training may become a question about an unavailable approval, then a question about a shared resource. Preserve the original episode and distinguish a new question from a synonym added for retrieval. Search translations and hypothetical answers help discover candidates; they do not become case facts. This field therefore combines prepared views with query-time interpretation and routes back to sources. The workload and available readers determine how much to prepare in advance and how much to interpret when a question arrives.

This distinction has a long information-retrieval lineage. Bates's 1989 account treats searching as an evolving inquiry that collects contributions through several techniques. It is a historical source for preserving the developing question. KCAE.SEARCH gives the present engineering construction; KCAE.Profiles compares current implementation choices.
