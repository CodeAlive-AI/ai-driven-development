## Engineering.Reference:7 - How should we set a target and judge a miss after conditions change?

A repair cooperative wants customers' equipment back in useful service, with accepted quality, agreed dates and enough means to keep operating. A manager proposes a completion target; Finance needs an honest forecast; the person allocating technicians needs a resource decision. Later, a missed target must inform an assessment. Treating one number as all four answers can make the work look coherent while its participants cannot act on it.

Use [OPS.21](../../management-dpf-suite/ops/55-ops-21---choose-operating-aims-targets-and-comparisons.md) to construct the aim and comparison when they are missing, [MA.5 through MA.6](../../management-dpf-suite/ma/12-ma-5---construct-and-update-an-operating-forecast.md) to obtain and preserve the conditional outlook, [OPS.13](../../management-dpf-suite/ops/46-ops-13---align-commitments-resources-and-service-outcomes.md) to establish actual service commitments, and [OPS.22](../../management-dpf-suite/ops/56-ops-22---interpret-operating-performance-and-decide-the-resp.md) to interpret the later result. Start with an adequate supplied contribution wherever one exists. **Choose the result worth pursuing and its comparison. Forecast what current conditions support. Find a feasible arrangement and establish what each party undertakes. Compare actual delivery with the aim and with the promises in force. Use that finding to settle the question or choose what to do next.**


### Engineering.Reference:7.1 - Construct the ambition from the receiving work

All quantities, evidence and authorities in this example are stipulated. Twelve identified customers have equipment that could usefully return to service this month through the cooperative's standard repair. Five have booked. Seven have qualified offers; an adequate nonoverlapping demand account supports three further acceptances under current conditions. The demand forecast is eight repair orders in all. Delivery capacity, order timing and acceptance of the repaired equipment enter the later forecast of completed work. The five bookings and the forecast of three further orders both refer to these twelve customers. The forecast does not identify which three will order.

OPS.21 begins before choosing an indicator. Useful restored equipment is the result. Counting closed tickets alone could reward incomplete repairs or avoiding difficult cases. The team therefore compares accepted repairs in this named customer population by month end, retaining quality evidence, unfinished cases and any later returns that could defeat the claim. Continuing service for existing recipients remains protected.

Eight, ten, twelve and fourteen now have different grounds. Eight is the expected number of orders. It leaves an expected gap of four relative to the twelve identified opportunities. How many repairs the cooperative can complete and customers will accept depends on the service arrangement. The base provision calculated below can support up to ten completed repairs under its stated conditions. Ten accepted repairs would serve two more needs than eight accepted repairs. The order forecast alone does not say how many repairs would otherwise be accepted. Twelve would address every identified suitable need if the customers choose repair and the cooperative obtains the means. Fourteen has no additional identified useful demand or way to serve it in this case. The team provisionally chooses twelve as an ambition, conditional on those needs remaining useful, customer choice and a worthwhile resource arrangement; its first action is to clarify the seven offers. This is a reasoned choice of ambition, not a claim that twelve is already likely or promised. A customer whose need disappears should not be sold an unwanted repair to protect the count.

The cooperative does not use a peer league table here: another team's mix, starting position and shared support have not been shown comparable. It can retain other observations without making each a new improvement target. A different operation could reasonably choose a range, a qualified milestone or observation without a numerical quota; OPS.21 explains those alternatives.


### Engineering.Reference:7.2 - Join the ambition to provision and dated money

The base team has 36 usable hours after separately protected continuing service. The stated work includes six setup hours plus three hours per standard repair, including completion and acceptance work. One obtainable staffed block adds eight usable hours for 180 monetary units. Using the operating capacity account, [MA.1](../../management-dpf-suite/ma/08-ma-1---build-the-resource-consumption-and-cost-model.md) and [MA.2](../../management-dpf-suite/ma/09-ma-2---explain-the-cost-and-use-of-capacity.md) give:

| Work under the initial standard-repair assumptions | Hours needed | Provision that could support it |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Eight repairs | 6 + 8 × 3 = 30 | The base 36 hours |
| Ten repairs | 6 + 10 × 3 = 36 | The base 36 hours, subject to calendar fit |
| Twelve repairs | 6 + 12 × 3 = 42 | One available block would give 44 hours |
| Fourteen repairs in a separate surge scenario | 6 + 14 × 3 = 48 | Two blocks would give 52 hours, if that demand and provision became relevant |

The resource holder and customer-facing coordinator examine the actual windows and shared assignments through OPS.13 and [OPS.19](../../management-dpf-suite/ops/53-ops-19---reconcile-simultaneous-operating-work-across-cases.md). Assume they establish the necessary calendar fit and qualification for the one-block arrangement. A forecast or suitable skill alone would not assign the technician. They keep the two-block surge request separate; the ambition does not justify buying it automatically.

The complete monthly cash boundary contains opening available cash of 850, base provision payments of 400, another existing obligation of 200 and materials of 20 per repair. A completed accepted repair brings a total customer receipt of 100. All stated outflows precede the day-25 receipts; no other material flows occur in this constructed boundary.

At eight repairs, payments are 400 + 200 + 160 = 760. Cash falls to 90 and then rises to 890 after receipts of 800. At twelve with one block, payments are 400 + 200 + 240 + 180 = 1,020. Ending cash of 1,030 would conceal an earlier shortage of 170. [FIN.2](../../management-dpf-suite/fin/15-fin-2---assess-liquidity-and-funding-needs-by-date.md) returns that dated funding condition before the dependent commitment.

The ten-repair alternative needs no extra block: payments are 800, the earlier cash minimum is 50 and closing cash is 1,050. Thus twelve would serve two more customers but leave 20 less in closing cash, because the extra receipts of 200 do not fully cover materials of 40 and the block of 180. The cooperative decides that the two additional restored pieces of equipment are worth that difference, subject to covering the earlier funding shortage and preserving continuing service. A cooperative unable or unwilling to bear the difference could choose ten and retain the two unmet needs for another response.

Suppose all twelve customers accept offers under which their repairs are due by specified dates this month. They agree to an advance totaling 300 within the contract price of 1,200 and pay it before the outflows. The responsible participants authorize and provide the staffed block. After the proposed outflows, cash would stand at 130, with 900 still to arrive on day 25 and ending cash of 1,030. The customers pay the same total price on different dates. Under MA.5 the cooperative can now forecast twelve completed repairs on the stated delivery and provision assumptions. Its earlier forecast was for eight orders. OPS.13 uses the accepted terms to establish what is now due to each customer and whose agreement a later date requires.


### Engineering.Reference:7.3 - Respond when the work changes and the new outlook is unwelcome

Before materials are bought, inspection shows that three of the twelve repairs need five hours each rather than three. The same count now requires 6 + 9 × 3 + 3 × 5 = 48 hours. Only 44 are available before the promised dates, and another block cannot arrive in that window.

Which dates could the cooperative still meet, and which customers could use a later slot? Deferring one three-hour repair leaves 45 hours of work. Deferring one five-hour repair would leave 43, so eleven repairs could fit the labour bound if their actual windows permit it. Suppose all three customers with complex repairs need their agreed dates. Two customers with standard repairs can use a later qualified window. The coordinator offers them those slots, verifies the schedule of the other ten and obtains both customers’ agreement. The ten now due this month need 6 + 7 × 3 + 3 × 5 = 42 hours. The two deferred cases retain their agreed work, payment and service conditions. The forecast changes with the arrangement. The ambition against which this month will be assessed remains twelve.

The financial account changes with the same arrangement. The extra block is retained and paid. Materials for the two deferred repairs, costing 40, are bought later. The agreed advance of 300 applies to the ten repairs still due this month, whose remaining receipts are 700 on day 25. Current outflows are 400 + 200 + 200 + 180 = 980; cash is 170 after them and 870 after the remaining receipts. The later account carries materials of 40 and receipts of 200 for the two deferred repairs, together with whatever provision that later work actually needs. It is not a completed gain in the current month. If materials had already been purchased or an advance had to be refunded, those different conditions would require a different dated account.

Now introduce a management conflict. The local review treats any downward forecast revision as poor performance, and the manager initially asks to keep reporting twelve. [MA.6](../../management-dpf-suite/ma/13-ma-6---separate-forecasts-targets-and-resource-requests.md) keeps the target, forecast, resource request and authorization distinct; [MA.9](../../management-dpf-suite/ma/16-ma-9---design-and-examine-the-use-of-management-accounting-i.md) exposes what the consequence rule encourages. Those distinctions need an effective response.

In this cooperative the manager may change the local review and recognition rule, while a separate committee retains decisions about pay. Using [OCE.10](../../management-dpf-suite/oce/32-oce-10---choose-a-response-to-participation-or-working-cultu.md), the manager changes the operative local instruction: retain the ambition and honest current outlook together; judge reporting on its grounds and timeliness; examine the response and actual result before assigning recognition. At the next review the manager accepts the supported forecast of ten, keeps twelve as the original ambition and uses the already agreed later bookings when checking provision for those repairs. Staff can now use the adverse forecast at the review where the old rule would have penalized reporting it. The committee retains its pay rule. If the manager lacked that power, the proposed rule change would have to reach its holder; an adviser or meeting facilitator could not supply the authority.


### Engineering.Reference:7.4 - Interpret the miss without losing accountability or the joint result

At month end ten repairs have been accepted, with the required quality, by the dates in the revised agreements. The ambition was twelve accepted repairs by month end, so it was missed. Two customers still have repairs due in the later window. Inspection had added six hours to the work originally forecast. The records of warning, agreement and subsequent delivery give OPS.22 grounds to recognize a response that secured service for the ten and kept the other two commitments. Fulfilling those later commitments will settle what the cooperative owes those customers.

A second team completed ten standard repairs against an ambition of eight. Its 125% and the first team's approximately 83% cannot decide which team performed better. If the question is whether these percentages justify ranking the teams, their unequal targets and work mixes already answer it. A comparison of the teams’ responses calls for the opportunities, decisions and shared support available to each. The first team's 42 hours and the standard team's 36 hours under the same setup premise show why the counts are not equivalent resource demands. They do not by themselves measure customer value, personal effort or a fair reward. An easy target does not prove deliberate manipulation either; its grounds need examination.

The manager can close the requested assessment. The cooperative missed its original ambition, fulfilled the ten agreements due this month and secured dates for the other two. The percentage figures leave the proposed ranking of the teams unsupported. The cooperative still owes the two repairs. If earlier diagnosis could improve future service, the technical lead can examine its value and cost. The committee can use the evidence of the operating response for a reward decision under its own rules. [HCD.11](../../human-capability-engineering-dpf-suite/hcd/28-hcd-11-assess-human-performance-in-representative-work.md) would require a different basis for a person's capability-development conclusion.

Apply the interpretation in both directions. If later evidence instead shows that the favorable count was obtained by taking another team's protected technician time without agreement, OPS.22 weakens that assessment and OPS.19 returns the displaced commitments for recovery. If all twelve needs remained useful but a feasible, worthwhile corrective action was knowingly left undone, context would not excuse that omission. If recipient needs changed, OPS.21 could revise the next aim while keeping the earlier result interpretable.

The methods can be used together or individually. A well-formed target can enter directly at the forecast or provision question; an assessment can begin with adequate existing observations. A budget, spending ceiling or external reporting date can remain wherever its function is needed. The test is whether the actual aims, accounts, decisions and consequences let participants improve the receiving work, including when the information is unwelcome.
