## HCE.Reference:3 - How do the contributions meet in a whole action?

Start from the work the recipient must do. In an account of a performance, follow the actions that made up the result and the conditions that allowed them to work together. In designing a method, determine which operations it calls for, which choices it leaves to the practitioner and which results others may supply. A teacher designing practice also needs to know which of those choices the learner must acquire. Before a first performance, compare the proposed practice with the choices learners must make and the help the design provides. To establish what someone actually chose during a performance, use evidence of that choice.

Choose the extent and the detail of the account together. Follow a part into the larger action when the answer depends on how the parts work together. A method can require a cue and let an external provider supply it. Trace how the cue affects the performance. Include the operation of producing it in the method only when the method calls for that operation or admits it as one way to meet the requirement. Across the range of work the recipient cares about, consider two arrangements the current description treats alike. Would they require different decisions or different explanations for the same result? If so, recover the difference at the point where it matters. An established relation or bound can already settle the question throughout the required range.

Suppose the order is to accept a pair's finish within half a beat of each other. A record saying “within one beat” fits a gap of a quarter of a beat and a gap of three quarters. Only the first passes. A reliable upper bound of a quarter of a beat settles acceptance for this performance without reconstructing every step. A teacher who wants to know whether one performer adjusted to the other needs a different account of that same finish. Both could have followed a count they already knew. Follow the cue through to the responding performer and find out whether the cue made a difference to their action. To explain this finish, connect the cue and the response in this duet. An earlier duet in comparable conditions can instead show what the performer could do and help the teacher choose the next assignment.

Consider the dancer in [HCD.Reference:4](../hcd/04-hcd-reference-4---several-contributions-can-be-simultaneous.md): the dancer has the required strength, can hold the starting stance without losing balance and remembers the figure. Balance fails during the turn. [SOM.Reference:4](../som/04-som-reference-4---which-actions-must-work-together-in-this-m.md) explains the simultaneous figure, rhythmic steps, changing support and bodily regulation. This is a constructed use of those full explanations, not an observed learning result.

The observation locates a difficulty in this turn. It leaves open whether to work on the dancer's coordination, the pair's timing or the working conditions. If the dancer already made this turn on time with a partner who signalled earlier, compare when each signal was available and what each dancer then did. A change in the pair's cue may make the figure usable with the skills both people already have. If evidence instead identifies a bodily coordination the dancer needs, [HCD.3](../hcd/18-hcd-3-diagnose-whether-a-capability-misconception-or-behavio.md) helps select that learning target. SOM.1 connects what the dancer experiences, can deliberately change and does.

SOM's full Methods provide different available constructions. [SOM.2](../som/10-som-2---make-a-bodily-change-distinguishable.md) is relevant when a needed change cannot be distinguished; [SOM.3](../som/11-som-3---regulate-bodily-effort-in-discernible-steps.md) when the increase or release of effort cannot yet be regulated; [SOM.4](../som/12-som-4---build-and-recall-a-coordinated-bodily-change.md) when available changes do not work together; and [SOM.5](../som/13-som-5---adapt-bodily-coordination-to-changing-support-and-lo.md) when support or load changes defeat the coordination. These are alternatives and returns within the repertoire.

HCD.2 includes the selected learning work together with the teaching, practice conditions and effort it needs. HCD.6 and HCD.10 connect preparation to the whole figure. When the dancer must respond to another person's changing timing, keep that interaction in the practice where the dancer makes the choice. A solo task can prepare the turn. To assess the response, use a duet that preserves the partner's actual choice and the dancer's opportunity to use it. If their job is to dance a prepared sequence to a recording, the recording can provide exactly the support their job calls for.

[HCD.11](../hcd/28-hcd-11-assess-human-performance-in-representative-work.md) supplies the performance judgement. [HCD.12](../hcd/29-hcd-12-test-transfer-to-unfamiliar-work-or-learning-from-a-n.md) and HCD.13 answer material transfer or retention questions when those are needed. A failed attempt at the whole action returns to the bodily, practice or support premise through HCD.14 and the relevant SOM Method. There is no need to complete all these evaluations when a smaller supported answer settles the current decision.

A different cause changes the route. Missing technique requires instruction; insufficient strength is not supplied by refining a sensation. A tool or surface change can make the action available without new bodily learning, as the writing case in [SOM.Reference:3](../som/03-som-reference-3---a-connected-worked-use.md) shows. Once the needed action works under its required conditions, further detail needs a reason. The teaching and safety conditions of the full SOM Methods continue to apply.
