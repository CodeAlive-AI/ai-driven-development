## MKT.12 - Investigate a Customer or Market Question for a Decision

> **Type:** Marketing Method pattern
> **Status:** Stable

### MKT.12:1 - Problem frame

Use this pattern when a decision about a product, offer or customer depends on understanding how work is done, how a choice is made, how an offering is used or what someone experienced. Customers might praise a service but stop using it, or a team might still be looking for a difficulty worth addressing. Start with the question the recipient needs answered. If the question or the alternatives needed for a choice have yet to take shape, examine a customer episode through §4.2–§4.3.

Give the recipient an answer with its limits or identify the question that remains open for their decision. That can come from existing information, ordinary information gathering, or a deliberately designed inquiry. A new survey is not the default result.

The intended reader understands the product or service and can arrange a competent conversation, observation and basic analysis. A specialised sampling, measurement or causal claim needs the corresponding expertise. This pattern supplies the marketing question and the connections between inquiry and action; it does not confer interviewing skill or access to private material.

Use an existing sufficient answer directly. Repair an already established service error through the responsible operating practice. If the decision concerns the effect caused by an intervention, qualify that causal question separately; a customer's account of an experience can inform it without answering it.

### MKT.12:2 - Problem

An organisation can collect abundant feedback without learning what its next decision requires. A buyer's stated preference may concern acquisition rather than use. A score after delivery may omit a difficulty that appears later. A striking interview may reveal a possible explanation while leaving its prevalence unknown. Combining these observations into one customer metric loses the question each can answer.

Construct the inquiry from the receiving decision and the available customer material. When the decision is still poorly understood, examining an actual attempt can first reveal the relevant difficulty and alternatives. When those are established, work backward to the answer needed, then forward from obtainable observations to a warranted answer. Distinguish who performed the work, who or what supplied each observation, and what you infer from it. Attribute a personal account to the person who gave it.

### MKT.12:3 - Forces

| Tension | Consequence for the inquiry |
| --- | --- |
| A decision needs an answer soon; the full situation may be extensive. | Seek the smallest answer that can distinguish the live actions or settle what the recipient can claim, and state what it leaves unresolved. |
| People can describe their situations; memory, incentives and question wording affect their reports. | Keep an attributed report separate from an observed event and an inferred explanation. |
| Common measures permit comparison; unlike meanings make the comparison misleading. | Establish a comparable activity, role, period and interpretation before aggregating. |
| Broad coverage costs attention and access. | Include a contrasting or missing case when it can change the conclusion, rather than collecting everything available. |
| The investigator may supply evidence for someone else’s decision or claim. | Identify the recipient and what they need to decide or substantiate. |

### MKT.12:4 - Solution

#### MKT.12:4.1 - Define the receiving question and try the existing answer

Identify who needs an answer, for what use and by when. If the question itself is unclear, or the recipient needs to choose among alternatives that have yet to emerge, examine an accessible episode through §§4.2–4.3 or the matching observation route. Recover the intended result, the operations and conditions, and a relevant contrast. This can reveal a difficulty or an option. Limit this exploratory work to the material available, its likely contribution and the burden of participation. Use what it establishes to refine the question.

For a choice among alternatives, identify which are still feasible and what would distinguish them. For a claim, identify what the recipient needs to establish. A team might have to choose between an easier acquisition route and a change to the service. It might instead need to establish whether the service reduced reporting time, even if it will continue the service either way. Use the available evidence first. Revise the question or stop collecting when no attainable answer could change the action or what the recipient can justifiably say.

Inspect available service records, previous inquiries, current customer accounts and relevant external sources. Check their subject, period and conditions before reuse. An old answer about individual buyers may not answer a current question about organisations with a separate access authority. Conversely, age alone does not defeat an answer whose conditions still hold.

Use sufficient information. When a missing fact can be obtained by a known suitable operation, obtain that fact and check its meaning. For a surviving non-routine knowledge question, take the question and existing grounds to [RMP.1](../../rmp/31-rmp-1-decide-whether-a-question-needs-research-and-when-to-s.md). Its qualification can return an existing answer, adjacent work, a missing-input hold or a research brief. Calling an activity customer research does not determine which result is appropriate.

#### MKT.12:4.2 - Identify the activity, participants and evidence needed

For the episode or continuing activity, identify who does the work, chooses the solution, pays, permits access and bears the effects. Establish each role and the source that can answer your question about it. A purchasing unit can choose a supplier. The company can pay, and an ordering service can place the order under a delegated rule. The order log can show which rule ran. An employee who found the process frustrating can describe that experience.

A customer can feel a bond with other users of a brand without meeting them. A profession or another group can also matter to the customer independently of the brand. A customer who associates the brand with that group may use it to express a sense of belonging. To investigate what the brand means to someone, ask which group they have in mind, when the connection mattered and what the brand expressed on that occasion. To study attachment, ask how the brand relates to their sense of self and how readily thoughts and feelings about it come to mind. [Carlson, Suter and Brown](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296307001774) · [Escalas and Bettman](https://people.duke.edu/~jrb12/bio/Jim/escalasbettman.pdf) · [Park and colleagues](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmkg.74.6.1)

Choose the inquiry that can answer the recipient's question.

| Question the recipient needs answered | Material and inquiry | Permitted first result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which results of an understood activity are poorly served? | Observe the work or inspect its records and establish the intended results. Where participants can explain a difficulty, ask about their experience. Compare the importance of each intended result and how well it is achieved where comparable measurement is possible. | Candidate improvements and differences between customers; use §4.3. |
| What does a product or brand mean to a customer in relation to a group? | Ask the person to describe the group and an occasion when the product mattered in that relationship. | An account of the meaning the person attributes to the product. |
| Why did the customer switch, decline or keep the existing alternative? | Reconstruct the decision, the alternatives available and the terms or preferences governing it. | A supported explanation or a specific uncertainty. An applicable procurement rule and the authorised record can establish why the organisation chose an option while leaving personal preference open. Use §4.4. |
| What happens at a contact, during continued use or around a new possibility? | Select observation, conversation and measures over the appropriate interval, with a named recipient. | A bounded account of experience or a new possibility; use §4.5. |
| How widespread is an established difference? | Define the population, unit, period and criterion. Use a complete register, a sufficient bound or a sampling design suited to the claim. | The number, threshold answer or estimate the grounds support, including uncertainty where relevant. |
| Did our intervention produce the change? | Distinguish the intervention, outcome, comparison and competing explanations; obtain the causal design needed for that claim. | A bounded causal conclusion, or a descriptive result when attribution remains unsupported. |

Use an account of brand meaning to identify what to investigate in a purchase. For a claim about why a particular purchase occurred, reconstruct who made the choice, which alternatives were available and what mattered under the conditions of that order (§4.4). To decide whether organizing a user group would strengthen attachment, compare what happens with a credible estimate of what would happen without that intervention (MKT.10). A one-time survey can find stronger attachment among active members even when people who already feel attached are more likely to participate. [Yu and Lin](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440261425574)

A technician calls a tool brand “ours” and explains that the workshop used it when training apprentices. For the current order the technician bought another brand. Of the two available, only that supplier offered the required warranty. Comparing the offers helps explain the purchase of the second.

An investigation of a purchase may reveal that the customer was trying to accomplish a different activity. A comparison of outcomes can identify an experience worth exploring in a conversation. Follow the new question with evidence suited to the conclusion the recipient needs.

#### MKT.12:4.3 - Investigate results of performing the work

Identify who performs the activity and describe it independently of a preferred product. Follow a concrete attempt through its trigger, intended result, actions, difficulties and outcome. Where a practitioner can describe the attempt, ask them to walk through it. Observe the work or inspect its permitted traces to establish what occurred and check the account. Separate a requested feature from the result it is meant to improve.

Use the attempt to sketch what must be accomplished for the activity to succeed. Start with its central work, then recover the preparation and completion it depends on. Ask what each part achieves and whether it is merely a feature of the present solution. "Open spreadsheet A" may serve "establish which source values are valid"; another way of performing the activity can obtain that result. A small map or list helps retain these relations. Keep the boundary tied to the decision instead of expanding into the performer’s other activities.

Challenge the account where a material difference could change the answer. An interrupted attempt, a rule governing handover or an obligation absent from the first episode may expose an omitted part of the work. Establish what was omitted, repeated or repaired and why it matters to the outcome. If a proposed addition belongs to another activity, show its connection to the original decision or agree to change the inquiry.

Turn a vague desire into a result that can be interpreted consistently. “Make reporting easy” might conceal time spent locating missing entries, risk of sending an incomplete report, or effort correcting discrepancies. State the direction of improvement, the thing affected and the relevant circumstances. Ask participants to explain the wording in their own situation; split an item when people are rating different things. A product-independent statement helps compare solutions, but it may still omit an important concern.

Check coverage before ranking the surfaced results. For each relevant part of the activity, determine whether the inquiry has a usable account of success, an unresolved question or a reason it cannot affect this decision. Ask whether a plausible missing outcome could change the target group, proposed contribution, next inquiry or what the evidence supports about the activity. Seek the smallest observation worth obtaining for that difference. If the observation is unavailable, identify the choice or claim that the evidence leaves unresolved, or narrow the conclusion to what it supports. Stop at this scope when further inquiry could change neither the next move nor the conclusion the recipient needs. Existing sufficient material can supply this comparison without a new mapping exercise.

Once meanings and coverage support the intended comparison, compare importance and present attainment for the participants and activity under consideration. Keep the two questions distinct. A result can be important and already adequately attained, or poorly attained but irrelevant to the current decision. Inspect combinations within and across cases before using an aggregate. A priority score is a decision aid under its assumptions; it does not prove demand, causal benefit or a stable segment.

Return candidate target results, the activity and contrasting attempts they cover, the relevant differences between executors and their evidence. Identify any unresolved omission that can change the receiving decision or limit the warranted comparison and the smaller answer needed to settle it. [MKT.1](16-mkt-1---qualify-a-market-problem-and-differences-between-cus.md) uses this to qualify customer groups; [MKT.2](17-mkt-2---construct-and-test-an-offer-and-its-positioning.md) uses it to construct an offer. If the activity or meaning of a measure changes, revisit that part of the comparison before carrying forward its ranking.

#### MKT.12:4.4 - Reconstruct a choice and the transition it involved

Choose a real episode and reconstruct the earlier situation, what prompted reconsideration, the alternatives considered, the decision and what followed. Use permitted records for the steps they can establish. In an interview, ask when the respondent first considered a change, which alternatives were available then and what made a transition attractive or difficult. Preserve uncertainty about dates or reasons.

Use concrete episodes and permitted documents to probe the account. “We chose it because it was modern” may lead to a more useful account of an upcoming deadline, a trusted colleague's advice, a procurement restriction or fear of losing a valued way of working. Functional, emotional and social considerations can coexist. Keep each attributed to the participant and supported at the strength available; do not diagnose motives that the inquiry cannot establish.

Include a contrasting case when it can defeat the explanation: someone who stayed, delayed, chose another supplier or did not complete a transition. Compare the circumstances that differ, not only the words people use. A possible explanation of several episodes can guide an offer or acquisition change. Estimating how often it applies needs additional grounds.

[MKT.4](19-mkt-4---make-choice-and-acquisition-meaningful-and-feasible.md) uses what the inquiry establishes about the transition difficulty. If the episode shows that the customer seeks a different result altogether, return to the problem and offer rather than treating every objection as a barrier to remove.

#### MKT.12:4.5 - Match the observation window to the experience question

For a specific transaction, collect information close enough to recover the relevant event and return the supported finding to the commissioned recipient within its permitted use. For a continuing relationship, observe use and changes over a period that can reveal the issue; a pleasant purchase does not settle later usefulness. For a new possibility, use a bounded exploration whose observations can change the proposed contribution. These are different choices of cadence and recipient, not obligatory successive phases.

Select the combination of observation, conversation and measures from what each can expose. A log may show that an action stopped without showing why. A conversation may explain a perceived difficulty without measuring its frequency. A rating records a response under particular wording and conditions. State what each contributes before joining them.

Schedule inquiry around the decision and the participant's burden. Combine requests when their meanings remain clear; stop repetitive questioning when it no longer changes the answer. If the assignment or an existing duty calls for urgent operational notice, alert the authorized recipient in time and disclose what that action requires.

#### MKT.12:4.6 - Obtain and interpret enough evidence

Choose evidence for the difference the decision depends on. A purposive set including relevant non-users can reveal a difficulty. To estimate a rate from a sample, define the population, selection process and measurement and explain the resulting uncertainty. A complete register can give the rate directly. With incomplete outcomes, a supported range can suffice for a threshold decision.

Arrange permitted access, understandable participation and competent inquiry. Pilot consequential questions on a small attainable case to find ambiguity before wider collection. Keep the question wording, relevant context and source of observations recoverable in the ordinary working material. Retain only information needed for the stated use under the applicable conditions. Obtain specialist advice where access, rights or a statistical claim depends on it.

During analysis, separate observed events, participant reports and your inferences. Compare cases and examine contrary observations. Ask whether a difference could come from the way information was obtained: selective response, changed wording, interviewer prompting, a different period or an incentive to give a favourable answer. A plausible explanation is useful as a hypothesis when it guides the next discriminating move; it need not be inflated into a general law.

#### MKT.12:4.7 - Return an answer that changes the work

Give the recipient the answer and its limits. Identify the activity, participants and conditions it covers, the sources for the conclusion, and what choice or claim the evidence supports. Name the next action or smaller question when one is needed. Make the grounds for a consequential inference available for examination. A concise note can suffice.

The answer can support proceeding, changing the offer, repairing supply, retaining an existing choice or stopping. It can also establish what the recipient may report about the service. If the intended use lacks a recipient or required authority, resolve that gap before committing to further measurement. Reopen only the affected question when customer work, roles, circumstances, measurement meaning or the decision changes.

### MKT.12:5 - Archetypal Grounding

#### MKT.12:5.1 - Positive onboarding scores, little subsequent use

Consider a stipulated supplier of a file-transfer service. The team wants to choose between simplifying its purchase form and changing the service. A support record already shows that one customer cannot obtain an access approval. Under the service agreement the support team can take that case to the party responsible for approval. The research comparison can use the permitted account of the failure without identifying the customer.

For the remaining question, the investigator follows permitted reporting attempts with an operator and reconstructs two contrasting buying episodes. Operators in both cases want less repeated entry. One purchaser can approve a short trial; the other needs a purchasing window and is reluctant to replace a familiar review process. The first observation concerns execution, the second set concerns acquisition and transition. They do not support one undifferentiated “easy to use” score.

The investigator also checks the work preceding entry against a month-end attempt with corrected source values. "Copy the spreadsheet" had hidden a preparation task: establish which values remain valid. The operator and the correction record confirm that a later revision can make an apparently complete report wrong. The additional desired result is reducing the chance of using a superseded value. It could change the service choice, so the inquiry adds that question rather than ranking duplicate-entry effort alone. The delivery owner must now establish whether the proposed operation can recognise a revised source or requires a separate customer check.

The returned answer is bounded: retain reduced entry and valid source selection as offer hypotheses, provide a separate acquisition route for the purchasing window, and investigate whether the familiar review can be retained. It does not estimate the market proportions of these cases. MKT.1 can now keep the source-validation and purchasing conditions visible, MKT.2 can construct a trial around those unresolved contributions, and MKT.4 can make its next decision feasible.

#### MKT.12:5.2 - Change the decision and the needed inquiry

Suppose management will continue the service and needs to know whether it can tell the customer that the trial reduced total reporting time. The earlier accounts do not identify that causal effect: workload or another process change may have altered the result. Define the comparison and competing explanations before choosing an evaluation design. The earlier observations help frame that comparison. Until the cause is established, the team can report what was observed within the cases studied.

In a learning service the employer may decide to enrol people and individual employees may use what they learn in a later job. To judge an employee’s readiness, establish which choices the job leaves to that person and what assistance will be available. Comparable earlier work can already answer that question. Credit learning or an effect of the course only when evidence connects the relevant change in capability to the period or intervention in question.

#### MKT.12:5.3 - Find the missing question before comparing offers

Suppose the same service team has only praise after onboarding and little subsequent use; it cannot yet formulate useful alternatives. With permission, it follows an operator's reporting attempt. Entry itself is quick, but the operator spends most of the attempt checking which source values have been superseded. A contrasting attempt uses already confirmed values and finishes without that repeated checking. These episodes reveal a candidate difficulty: obtaining valid inputs, rather than entering them faster.

The team can now compare supplying source validation with retaining the existing validation arrangement, and ask which version information each requires. The observations justify that local question; they do not establish its market prevalence or the causal effect of a new service. If the relevant source history cannot be obtained, retain the discovered difficulty and leave the affected offer comparison unresolved. The specification or a demonstrated use of an existing validation service may already show which version information it requires; use that answer without another interview.

### MKT.12:6 - Bias-Annotation

Easy-to-reach customers, recent complaints and vivid successful transitions can dominate the account. Look for a missing case that could reverse the decision or narrow the conclusion. A supplier can also prefer the question its existing dashboard can answer. Begin with the decision before deciding which available measure to use.

An investigator, including a service working with authorized material, can select a contrast, compare accounts and draw an inference. Keep the claims connected to the sources and inspect meaning, disagreement and omitted cases. A buyer’s account can support what that buyer experienced. If you need to credit the investigation, establish who selected the contrast, drew the inference or independently checked it.

### MKT.12:7 - Conformance Checklist

- The reader can identify the receiving decision or claim, any live alternatives, and what evidence could settle the question.
- Existing sufficiency, ordinary information gathering and non-routine inquiry remain distinct possible outcomes.
- The activity, participants, period and sources support the stated conclusion. For an outcome comparison, check which parts of the activity may have been missed and whether the answer is sufficient for its intended use or a material question remains open.
- Result investigation, choice history, experience observation and causal evaluation are not silently substituted for each other.
- Contrary evidence, relevant missing cases, participant burden and conditions of data use can change the inference or next action.
- A named recipient receives the answer, its limits and an actionable continuation or stop.

### MKT.12:8 - Common Anti-Patterns and How to Avoid Them

| Misuse | Repair |
| --- | --- |
| Ask what features people want, then call the list their underlying work. | Recover the actual attempt and expected result before deciding which feature could contribute. |
| Accept the first answer to “why?” as a complete account. | Reconstruct the choice and its circumstances. Use an applicable decision rule, the decision record or a contrasting episode according to what still needs explanation. |
| Add more post-transaction ratings to explain long-term non-use. | Choose an observation window that includes use and the relevant outside conditions. |
| Turn a few interviews into a market percentage or intervention effect. | Keep the explanatory hypothesis; obtain the distinct design required for the stronger conclusion. |

### MKT.12:9 - Consequences

The team can obtain a useful answer without automatically commissioning a study. When inquiry is needed, its effort goes toward a distinction that changes the decision or the conclusion the recipient can support. This can also reveal that no answer is presently obtainable within the access, competence or time available. Preserve that limit in the decision rather than reporting certainty because a questionnaire was completed.

### MKT.12:10 - Architectural Rationale

The decision-to-evidence-to-action connection belongs together because an otherwise competent data-collection operation can answer the wrong question. Different inquiry routes remain available because their units and warranted conclusions differ. They can be composed through explicit new questions without a universal sequence or a single meaning of “job”.

A full research method, specialist measurement or causal design remains with its supplier. The local contribution is selecting and interpreting those results for a marketing decision, including the sufficient-answer stop and the return to a changed offer or service.

### MKT.12:11 - SoTA-Echoing

For choosing an inquiry, the selected line is question-specific construction with explicit limits. **Adapt** [Strategyn's public ODI account, steps 1–5](https://strategyn.com/outcome-driven-innovation/) for comparing results of an identified activity, and the [Christensen Institute's current JTBD account](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/) for reconstructing choice in circumstances. They are useful rival entries for different questions, not interchangeable definitions. This changes §§4.2–4.4. The linked [needs explanation](https://strategyn.com/outcome-driven-innovation/understanding-customer-needs/) and [job-map construction](https://strategyn.com/jobs-to-be-done/customer-centered-innovation-map/) inform the coverage challenge in §4.3; stop when further evidence could no longer change the choice or the warranted conclusion at the intended scope. Supplier success claims, permanent outcome stability and population-wide causation are not adopted. Reopen the choice when the activity or explanation no longer fits the observed cases.

For cadence and receiving action, **adapt** [Meyer and Schwager, 2007, pp. 6–11](https://zimmer.csufresno.edu/~sasanr/Teaching-Material/MIS/SIS/understanding-customer-experience.pdf): transaction, relationship and exploratory questions call for different arrangements. This historical construction remains useful in §4.5 because a uniform survey misses their different time spans. Keep the later [Becker and Jaakkola, 2020, premises 1, 2 and 4](https://pure.hanken.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/42844202/2020_Becker_Jaakkola.pdf) distinction between provider conditions and a customer's response. The added design effort is worthwhile when distinguishing those conditions from the customer’s response could improve the decision or establish what the recipient may claim. Use ordinary information when it suffices. These sources do not establish the effectiveness of this combined method.
