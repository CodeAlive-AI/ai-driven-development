## CB.12 - Support a Useful Repeated Action of Participation

> **Type:** Community-building method pattern
> **Status:** Stable

### CB.12:1 - Problem frame

Use this pattern when a person wants to repeat a useful action of participation, but the intended action does not reliably happen in the situations where it would help. A practitioner forgets to return the result of a peer consultation. A volunteer wants to check a shared resource after using it but cannot fit the step into their working conditions. Reminders increase visits while the wanted contribution remains unfinished.

The wider subject of habit change includes many personal and professional behaviours. This pattern addresses the narrower construction of a voluntarily supported, useful repeated action in community participation. It connects a recognisable occasion, a feasible action, its useful return and the conditions that let the person continue or stop. The professional correctness of the action and the development of missing capability come from their direct suppliers.

**First useful result:** the person and organiser have a feasible arrangement for one wanted action and can examine what happens at subsequent relevant opportunities, including an unmet condition or a decision to discontinue the arrangement.

A habit involves a learned tendency to respond in a particular context with less need for a fresh deliberation to begin or perform the action. Repeated attendance alone does not establish that tendency. A dependable deliberate practice or a retained reminder can be an adequate result when automaticity is unnecessary.

**Ordinary non-use.** Keep an already workable routine. Use a simple accessible route for a rare, irregular need when the person can find it at the right time. Do not create repeated participation to compensate for an absent benefit, unavailable helper or unsuitable obligation. A different behavioural subject calls for its appropriate professional method.

### CB.12:2 - Problem

A successful first encounter does not necessarily make the useful action easy to repeat. The person may fail to notice the relevant situation, know what to do but lack material or time, begin but not finish, or discover that the expected result no longer matters.

An organiser who sees only a participation count can respond to all of these with more reminders or rewards. That can increase visible activity while leaving the actual obstacle unchanged.

How can the organiser and participant build and revise a useful repeated action, distinguish what prevents it, and retain the person's ability to choose another way or stop?

### CB.12:3 - Forces

| Force | Decision it changes |
| --- | --- |
| An easy return and a worthwhile result | Lowering effort helps only if the action still produces something the person or receiving work values. |
| A stable occasion and changing circumstances | A familiar context can support repetition; a changed schedule, tool or responsibility may need another arrangement. |
| Support and dependence | A reminder or helper can be appropriate continuing support; withdrawing it tests a different condition and may impair the result. |
| Visible repetition and uncertain mechanism | Counts show occurrences, while initiation, completion, usefulness and automaticity answer different questions. |
| Continued participation and voluntary choice | A person may value a practice now and reasonably reduce or end it later. |

### CB.12:4 - Solution

Choose one action worth repeating. Examine its actual opportunities, beginning, completion and return; repair the relevant conditions; try a proportionate arrangement with the participant; and use later episodes to retain, revise or end it.

**Working reminder:** choose the useful action; locate the failed connection; make a feasible occasion and response; try it; inspect useful repetitions and burden; change or stop what no longer serves the person.

#### CB.12:4.1 - Agree what is worth repeating

Begin with the participant's receiving need and a concrete action. “Visit the community more” gives no reason to prefer one visit to another. “When a peer's answer has been tried, return the result so that the advice can be corrected” names useful work and a relevant occasion.

Establish what would make one episode complete. The result may be an applicable answer found, a permitted question sent, a contribution returned to its recipient or a bounded period of valued shared activity. It need not be a public post.

Ask whether repetition is appropriate. A daily work handover and an occasional unusual case have different opportunities. The frequency of a platform notification is not the frequency of the person's need. If an address or bookmark is enough for a rare event, preserve that simpler arrangement.

Agree what support the person wants, the burden they can accept and when the arrangement should be reconsidered. Distinguish their voluntary participation from a duty already accepted through employment, membership or another agreement. Explain such a duty through its actual basis; adding a community reminder does not create or remove it.

For a shared activity, obtain the other participants' contributions too. One person's intention to seek help does not guarantee that a qualified respondent will be available. CB.5 supplies the contribution and return arrangement.

#### CB.12:4.2 - Reconstruct what happens at an actual opportunity

Use a recent episode, or a short observation of the next relevant one. Recover the situation in which the action would have helped, what the person noticed, what they began, what they could complete and what they received.

Keep four questions distinct:

| Question | An observation that can change the next move |
| --- | --- |
| Did a relevant opportunity occur and become noticeable? | The intended situation never occurred, was not recognised, or a notification arrived at an unusable time. |
| Could the person begin the wanted action? | The entry was hard to find, the request was unclear, or required material and permission were absent. |
| Could they carry the action through? | They started but lost time, access, assistance or an applicable method before completion. |
| Did the episode supply its intended value? | The answer arrived too late, the activity failed its purpose, or the person no longer wanted that result. |

Ask for the participant's account rather than inferring a motive from silence. A person who reads an applicable answer without posting may have completed the wanted action. A daily visitor may still be unable to obtain help.

Where an explanation remains uncertain, choose a small change or observation that can distinguish the live alternatives. For example, making the return link available at case closure can test a findability problem; sending more reminders while the response team is absent tests no useful repair. Preserve the other relevant conditions so that the observation remains interpretable, and keep its conclusion local.

If the expected benefit is unclear, ask what the person hoped the episode would settle and what remained afterwards. Compare plausible results rather than selecting a reward in advance. A maintained explanation may settle one question; another needs a peer to inspect unfamiliar conditions; a desired social encounter can have a different useful result. Agree a small trial of the relevant alternative using the applicable guidance in CB.1 and CB.5, then use the person's account and the receiving result to decide whether the original action is still worth repeating.

Compare episodes that led to useful action with ones that did not. Look for the event that made the need apparent and the conditions in which the person could respond. Distinguish a potentially useful cue from a merely coincident feature: a notification may have arrived every day, while the actual occasion was completion of a case. Choose the next observation to separate the plausible explanations; treat an unresolved pattern as uncertainty rather than a personal defect.

HCD.3 supplies a fuller comparison when a possible human capability limitation is the receiving question. It compares that explanation with method, access, support, organisation and measurement problems. Use its result rather than diagnosing a lack of motivation whenever participation falls.

#### CB.12:4.3 - Build an occasion, action and return that fit together

Choose an occasion the participant can recognise in their actual work. It may be an existing event, a relevant state of the case or a selected point in the day. Explain the action in terms that remain intelligible there. A person finishing a diagnostic comparison needs the route for returning that comparison, not an unrelated invitation to browse.

Make the beginning easy without deleting the work needed for a useful result. Put the relevant reference, permitted question route or return location where it is needed. Pre-fill stable information when that is appropriate, while leaving the person to inspect and supply the case-specific content. A one-click response is inadequate if the receiving use needs a distinction it omits.

Prepare completion conditions as well as the starting cue. The person may need a quiet interval, available material, usable access or a respondent within a certain window. Walk through one episode under those conditions. If the expected response cannot be supplied, change the promise or obtain provision before inviting repetition.

Specify a realistic fallback. When the planned time disappears, use another feasible occasion if that still serves the result. When the case contains protected information, use an authorised route. When the wanted result is no longer relevant, omit the action. A fallback should preserve the purpose rather than preserve a participation count.

A short personal plan can connect the recognised situation, chosen action, expected result and fallback. Put it where the participant will use it.

The useful return should be dependable enough for the promise being made. An answer, clarified case, valued exchange or maintained reference can supply that return. An added prize is not required when the activity already gives the person the result they want.

#### CB.12:4.4 - Choose support and useful accumulation deliberately

Use a reminder when remembering or noticing is the actual difficulty and the person wants that support. Agree its channel and timing, what action it should make intelligible, and how to change or end it. Combine or remove messages that compete for the same attention.

Begin with enough support for a feasible episode. Help can include a prepared example, a companion for an unfamiliar first attempt or a reliable route back to the relevant material. CB.3 supplies attainable entry and preparation; HCD supplies learning where the required action has not yet been learned.

Do not equate success without a reminder with success without every kind of support. An accessible instruction, an accommodation or an available professional can remain necessary. If independence from a particular prompt is worth testing, agree a safe, useful opportunity to reduce that prompt while preserving the other conditions. Keep the support if its continuing value justifies it.

Let repeated use leave something helpful when that serves a later need: a corrected explanation, a relevant saved example, an agreed connection or a useful personal setting. Make that accumulated result obtainable for its receiving use and preserve the relevant permissions. Its purpose is to improve the next episode; sunk effort alone is no reason to make leaving harder.

An organiser may also recognise a contribution through CB.6. Keep that response tied to the contribution and the person's preferences. A recognition programme is neither a necessary condition for repetition nor evidence that a habit has formed.

#### CB.12:4.5 - Observe the next episodes and distinguish the result

Choose an observation opportunity from the action's real occurrence and the decision being made. A daily task can produce several observations soon; an uncommon fault cannot. Do not manufacture irrelevant activity to fill an observation calendar.

Retain only information needed to decide what to change. Ask whether the relevant occasion occurred, whether the person noticed and acted, what support was present, whether the intended result was obtained, and what burden the episode imposed. A missed occasion, a justified decision not to act and an unfinished attempt should not become the same failure count.

Compare the episodes with the expected connection. If the person notices the occasion but cannot finish, repair completion conditions. If they complete the action but receive no useful result, repair the contribution or reconsider the action. If they do not notice an otherwise feasible occasion, change the cue or agreed support. If the person no longer wants the result, reconsider the arrangement with them.

A person's report that the action now comes to mind readily can inform a local adjustment. Ask separately how readily the occasion brings the action to mind and how much deliberate effort is still needed to carry it through. Compare these accounts across relevant episodes, keeping the actual support visible. When it matters, combine them with an agreed observation under reduced prompting. This can guide the next local trial; it is not a numerical habit-strength assessment. A stronger automaticity claim needs an appropriate measurement method and qualification for the intended use. Frequency alone cannot supply it.

Likewise, a favourable change after introducing the arrangement is useful local evidence but does not by itself establish a causal effect or transfer to every participant. Preserve the actual conditions when deciding what can be reused.

#### CB.12:4.6 - Retain, revise, transfer or stop

Retain the arrangement when it supplies a wanted result at acceptable burden. Fewer reminders, fewer visits or a simpler action can be an improvement if the receiving result is preserved.

Revise the part that failed. A new work schedule may need another occasion; a changed platform may need another route; a different question may need another respondent. Keep the parts that still work. If a change couples several conditions, avoid claiming that the observation isolates one cause.

After absence or a role change, recover the current need and conditions before restarting the old routine. Use CB.3 when the person is returning or changing their contribution. A former commitment can be reduced or replaced; the person need not recover an old activity level merely to count as returned.

Where continued professional development is the purpose, HCD.15 supplies the fuller arrangement for later-work triggers, contribution ownership, evidence and reassessment. Its professional criteria govern that result. A repeated community action can support the arrangement without establishing capability by itself.

Stop the support when the person declines it, the result no longer matters, the burden is unacceptable or the necessary contribution cannot be provided. Turn off the agreed reminders, clarify any remaining accepted commitment and preserve access to useful material where its terms allow. If another person has relied on a continuing contribution, arrange a handover or revise that promise through its owner.

On closing the arrangement, explain what remains available, what has ended and how the person can return if a new need arises.

### CB.12:5 - Archetypal Grounding

#### CB.12:5.1 - Returning the result of peer advice

In a constructed repairer association, participants want to return what happened after using peer advice. The returned cases help respondents correct the shared explanation. Useful case outcomes often remain unreported after the immediate repair work ends.

The organiser and one willing participant examine recent cases. Some advice has not yet been tried, so those cases have no result to return. In completed cases, the person remembers the discussion but must search for its location. The repair is to keep a link to the permitted discussion with the case material and place the return at the point where the case outcome is recorded.

One episode can be complete with a short account of what was tried, the resulting condition and any unresolved limit. Confidential details stay in the authorised route. The organiser confirms that a respondent will inspect the return and revise the explanation if necessary.

At later relevant closures, the participant can use the link without a separate platform reminder. This supports retaining the simpler arrangement. It shows useful returns under those conditions; it does not establish a general habit-strength value or a community-wide causal effect.

If the participant begins the return but lacks a confirmed outcome, the remedy is to wait for or obtain that information.

#### CB.12:5.2 - A rare need requires access, not daily participation

An independent repairer occasionally encounters an unfamiliar configuration. They want to obtain a bounded review when the available instruction does not settle the case. The organiser initially proposes a daily digest to keep the community prominent.

The repairer instead needs an easy-to-find request route, a permitted way to prepare the case and a realistic response window. CB.5 helps obtain the relevant respondent. The route is saved with the working reference.

The repairer does not visit on days when no such question occurs. When a suitable case does arise, they use the route. A later case needing an urgent response uses the accountable service instead. The arrangement has improved access to help without adopting a daily habit as its objective.

If no one can answer the relevant class of question, the organiser changes the promised scope.

#### CB.12:5.3 - A changed schedule interrupts completion

A volunteer has been checking a shared reference for one obsolete link after a regular weekly session. A change of schedule removes the quiet interval in which that work was feasible. A reminder still arrives, and the volunteer often opens the reference, but the correction remains unfinished.

They distinguish remembering from completion. The volunteer chooses a different available interval and keeps the bounded task. A maintainer remains available for corrections that the volunteer cannot judge or publish. The next episode supplies a checked correction without increasing the number of reminders.

If the task itself has changed and the person cannot assess the material, the community obtains the needed subject judgement or learning contribution.

#### CB.12:5.4 - End a reminder while preserving the useful result

A member uses a general daily notification to find occasional answers. It increasingly interrupts their work and draws them into unrelated conversations. They prefer to consult the maintained reference when their own question arises.

The organiser helps remove the notification and makes the reference route easy to recover. The member retains permitted access and can submit a question if the reference is insufficient. Their visible activity decreases while the wanted use remains available.

In another case, the person has also agreed to inspect a shared resource on a recurring basis. They still want to perform that task and choose a different workable reminder. If they also decide to stop the contribution, they agree a revised promise or handover with its recipient.

#### CB.12:5.5 - Find out what makes a recurring exchange worth using

In a constructed group of workshop volunteers, a member says that the weekly exchange helps them "keep up", yet often skips it. The organiser has two plausible explanations: the member wants a concise update on changed practices, or wants a second person's judgement before using a practice in their own work. These suggest different recurring actions: reading a digest or bringing an unsettled case.

They compare recent episodes. The member read an update at home and missed the meeting after finding what they needed. On another occasion, they attended after receiving the same update, asked about an unfamiliar material and stayed until someone helped them decide the next step. A third visit was enjoyable but left the working question unresolved. The member's account favours getting judgement on an actual case; the episodes also leave room for valued social contact.

For the next suitable weekly exchange, the member agrees to try a short inspection of their pending case with a competent respondent. The usual invitation, time and place remain; the first part of the meeting is reserved for that case instead of the general round of updates. They judge the trial by whether the member leaves with a usable next step and its limits. If the reference alone settles the question beforehand, they can report that and release the slot.

The trial produces a bounded next step that the member can use. They choose to retain the digest for updates and, at their regular preparation of workshop work, check whether an unsettled case merits a request for the next exchange. The organiser keeps a respondent available for that promised use.

This supplies a practical reason to separate the two actions and occasions. It does not establish that case inspection caused attendance: the participant expected special attention, and personal contact may still matter. A later episode in which a case remains unresolved, no respondent is available or the member wants company rather than advice changes the next arrangement.

### CB.12:6 - Bias-Annotation

An organiser sees visits and messages more easily than usefulness, unwanted interruption or effort outside the platform. The method starts from the person's action and its receiving result. A successful return by a keen participant also says little about people with different opportunities, access or support; keep those conditions visible when considering another application.

### CB.12:7 - Conformance Checklist

A useful repeated-action arrangement establishes:

- an action the person wants or has accepted, with an intelligible result;
- real opportunities for that action;
- the current difficulty in noticing, beginning, completing or obtaining value;
- feasible conditions, wanted support and a useful fallback;
- observations that can change the arrangement without equating frequency with habit;
- an understandable way to retain, revise, reduce or stop the support and any affected commitment.

A feasible first episode is enough to try the arrangement. A claim about automaticity, durable capability, causal benefit or continued performance under different support requires evidence suited to that claim.

### CB.12:8 - Common Anti-Patterns and How to Avoid Them

| Wrong turn | Practical repair |
| --- | --- |
| Choose daily participation before identifying the recurring need | Establish the real occasion and consider a simple accessible route. |
| Treat every missed action as weak motivation | Distinguish absent opportunity, unnoticed occasion, unavailable conditions and an unwanted result. |
| Make starting easy but leave completion unsupported | Obtain the necessary material, time, method and receiving response. |
| Increase reminders when nobody can supply the promised help | Repair provision or change the promise. |
| Report visits or a streak as a formed habit | Preserve the occurrence claim and obtain an appropriate basis for automaticity if it matters. |
| Withdraw a useful support merely to prove independence | Test only the condition the receiving use needs, with agreement and an adequate fallback. |
| Keep a person returning because they have already invested effort | Preserve useful access and make a new choice from current value and burden. |
| Treat stopping as failure despite a better alternative | End the support and settle any remaining accepted contribution. |

### CB.12:9 - Consequences

The organiser and participant can locate a failed connection and change it without escalating all reminders, rewards or activity targets. Useful repetition can coexist with quiet use, continued support and a reduced frequency of visits.

Observation and support still require effort. Rare opportunities may leave the mechanism uncertain for a long time. An adequate deliberate practice can therefore be a better objective than proving automaticity, and a simpler access arrangement can be a better result than a habit programme.

### CB.12:10 - Rationale

Repeating an action requires more than recalling its beginning. Material, time, access, other people's contributions and the returned value can differ from one occasion to the next. Separating these conditions gives the organiser a useful repair even when the psychological mechanism is not established.

The person also retains a reason to choose whether the action should continue. An arrangement that is easy to repeat can outlast the need it once served. Inspecting value and burden alongside repetition prevents continuation from becoming its own objective.

### CB.12:12 - Relations

- **Uses CB.1 and CB.5:** a worthwhile result, an available contribution and its returned use.
- **Uses CB.3 and CB.4:** attainable participation and a setting in which the action can be completed.
- **Combines with CB.6:** a meaningful acknowledgement where useful.
- **Uses [HCD.3 (HCD.3:4)](../../human-capability-engineering-dpf-suite/hcd/18-hcd-3-diagnose-whether-a-capability-misconception-or-behavio.md#hcd34---solution):** diagnosis when a possible human limitation must be compared with conditions and non-training explanations.
- **Uses [HCD.15 (HCD.15:4)](../../human-capability-engineering-dpf-suite/hcd/32-hcd-15-sustain-continuing-human-capability-development.md#hcd154---solution):** a continuing professional-development arrangement when later capability is the result.
- **Returns to CB.1:** a different contribution or simpler route now serves the person better.
