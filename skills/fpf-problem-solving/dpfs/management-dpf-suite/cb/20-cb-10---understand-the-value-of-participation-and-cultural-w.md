## CB.10 - Understand the Value of Participation and Cultural Work

> **Type:** Community-building method pattern
> **Status:** Stable

### CB.10:1 - Problem frame

Use this pattern when people need to decide whether a cultural or community arrangement is useful, what to change, or what support to continue, and visible activity does not answer that question. Meetings may be well attended while the intended help remains absent. A quiet reader may use a guide successfully without posting. An employer may ask what changed in practice after staff spent time together.

The subject is the value and burden of the arrangement for its participants, contributors, independent users and supporters. The move is to recover particular experiences and uses, connect them with relevant observations, and obtain an account that informs a practical decision. This includes cultural work whose carriers do not communicate with one another.

The first useful result states what people received or did, what changed, which contribution the evidence supports, what remains uncertain and which condition should be continued or changed. A useful conversation or sense of welcome can be a result in its own right. A promise of improved work also needs evidence from that work.

The intended user can talk with affected people and obtain or commission the observations needed for the decision. The pattern develops practical inquiry and feedback. A causal estimate, specialist capability judgement or formal method trial requires the corresponding method and evidence.

**Ordinary non-use.** When the result is already established at the needed scope and the only difficulty is allocating the agreed support, use CB.9. A routine attendance count needed to book a suitable room can remain a routine count. Return here when its interpretation or the activity's usefulness is in question.

### CB.10:2 - Problem

The easiest observations often concern the organiser's activity: events held, messages posted or resources published. The benefit may occur later, elsewhere and through a combination of contributions. People can enjoy a session without using its advice, use a resource without telling its author, or obtain an important result from one brief exchange.

Stories and numbers have complementary limits. A general success story can omit the action that supposedly helped. An indicator can change because the people, work or conditions changed. Listening only to those who continue participating can hide exclusion, unsuccessful use and the cost of leaving.

How can an organiser investigate the contribution and its limits well enough to improve the arrangement, while keeping the inquiry proportionate and useful to those who supply its evidence?

### CB.10:3 - Forces

| Force | Decision it changes |
| --- | --- |
| Visible activity and value elsewhere | Easy counts help locate work but can miss later independent use. |
| Participants' meanings and comparable observations | An account must retain what matters to people while supporting the comparison needed for the decision. |
| A convincing story and a warranted contribution claim | A detailed sequence can make a contribution plausible without identifying its causal effect. |
| Learning and accountability | Supporters need an account, while excessive reporting can consume the participation being supported. |
| Positive use and missing voices | Available successes can conceal non-use, harm, burden or people who could not enter. |
| Stable observation and newly discovered value | A consistent indicator supports comparison; an unexpected use may require a different question. |

### CB.10:4 - Solution

Begin with the practical decision, follow specific experiences into their receiving use, and compare the account with observations that could confirm, limit or contradict it. Return the result to participants as a reason for a concrete next move.

**Working reminder:** choose the decision; recover an occurrence; follow its use; compare evidence and missing cases; change a condition; return and observe the result.

#### CB.10:4.1 - Name whose decision and whose value matter

Recover the question with the person who will use the result. Continuing a monthly exchange, correcting an invitation, funding a guide and claiming reduced service cost require different evidence. Name the period and the change being considered, including leaving an adequate arrangement alone.

Ask affected people what would count as useful and what would count as an unacceptable burden. Keep differences visible. A participant may value a dependable answer, an organiser a manageable workload, and a sponsor a competent workforce. A gain for one party can coexist with a loss for another.

Distinguish the claims before selecting observations. Useful questions can concern experience and inclusion, relationships, access to a resource, ability to perform a practice, actual use, results in that use, or the provision and governance of the arrangement. Choose the question that matters to the receiving decision.

For a cultural result, use the distinctions in CB.1 and [FPF C.36](../../../sections/09-part-c---kernel-extension-specifications/91-c-36---cultural-evolution-and-cultural-evolution-engineering.md): people carrying a practice, their opportunities to enact it and the continuation of the practice are related but different subjects. A registration or download count alone does not establish any person's capability or later use.

Choose further inquiry only when an attainable answer could improve the decision enough to justify the burden of obtaining and using it. An account of the last failed entry may suffice to repair a confusing invitation. A claim about programme-wide value needs a broader and appropriately selected evidence base. State the scope before a vivid case becomes a statement about everyone.

#### CB.10:4.2 - Choose observations and people who can answer

Find where the promised result would become visible. A participant can report whether they felt welcome; a recipient can show whether a handover let them act; an operating record can show later completion or repeated help requests. Use an existing adequate observation when its meaning, period and access fit.

An indicator needs a recoverable unit and population. For returning participation, distinguish people from visits and say who could have returned during the period. For response, distinguish an initial acknowledgement from a useful answer. If the observation method changes, retain that difference before interpreting a trend.

Use activity patterns to find informative accounts. Include a relevant contrast: someone who returned, someone who left after one attempt, a quiet user, or a contributor whose work stopped. Select the contrast because it can change the explanation. A few purposeful conversations can expose a repair without representing the whole population.

Provide an appropriate way to answer. People may need a private conversation, an accessible format or someone other than their organiser to receive criticism. Explain how the account will be used, obtain permission for attributed reuse and retain only detail needed for the inquiry. A refusal or missing response remains missing evidence.

Keep collection proportionate. Start with a small number of useful observations and cases; expand when plausible alternatives still change the decision. If the question needs specialist sampling or causal identification, obtain that contribution instead of treating more informal interviews as an equivalent.

#### CB.10:4.3 - Recover the path from experience to use

Ask for one particular occasion, preferably in the person's own account. Recover what happened, what the person received, what they subsequently did with it and what difference that made. A story can begin with a later result and work backwards. Check time order and retain where the account ends.

For social learning, the Guidebook's four basic value cycles help ask different questions:

| Part of the account | Useful recovery question | What the answer can establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Immediate experience | What happened in the exchange, and what did it mean to you then? | A reported experience, such as useful discussion or being able to ask a difficult question. |
| Potential resource | What idea, contact, explanation or other resource became available? | Something that could support later work, with its actual content or availability. |
| Applied use | What did you do differently, where and under which conditions? | An account of adoption or adaptation, including failed use. |
| Realised difference | What changed in the receiving situation, and what was that worth to those affected? | A reported or observed result and burden, qualified by the other evidence. |

Follow each consequential connection. If someone says the meeting improved a repair, ask which contribution they used and how it changed the action. If they received an idea but have not had an occasion to use it, keep that as potential value. Agree a later return only when it can answer the decision.

Recover the conditions that enabled or altered the path. The Guidebook distinguishes enabling value, such as obtaining time and support for practice, from strategic value, such as a conversation that connects the community's contribution with a stakeholder's decision. Understanding a relevant external development can provide orienting value. Transformative value concerns a change in participants' identity or the systems and culture around their practice, such as an adopted change to an organisation's rules. Follow these additional contributions when they help explain the use or its limits.

Ask about cost, unwanted effects and failed attempts with the same specificity. What extra preparation was needed? Who could not use the resource? Which expected result did not occur? A useful negative account can show where the proposed method or support fails.

Keep the person's experience and the investigator's interpretation distinguishable. Let the contributor correct an attributed account before it is circulated. If the purpose is simply a valued social experience, the immediate account can answer it; inventing a later productivity gain would change that purpose.

#### CB.10:4.4 - Compare the account with other observations

Seek the observation most capable of changing the account's meaning. It may be a recipient's response, an actual work product, an existing activity record or a relevant outcome indicator maintained for another purpose. Recover how it was obtained and whether it concerns the same people, work and period.

Compare the parts that can conflict. A participant may report that an answer resolved the issue while a later request shows that a necessary step remained unclear. A reported improvement may coincide with a different workload or new tool. Ask what the discrepancy changes; do not discard it to preserve the preferred story.

Use stories to interpret indicators and indicators to challenge stories. An unexpected rise in repeat requests can identify a case worth following. An unexpected successful use may reveal a benefit that the current indicator misses. Revise the question or indicator when the receiving decision needs that new meaning, retaining enough continuity to interpret the change.

Treat absent links explicitly. When a result is known but the supposed community contribution cannot be recovered, report the result without attributing it. When a contribution was used but no later outcome can yet be observed, return the use and the remaining question. Lack of a record is not automatically lack of value; it limits what the account can support.

[A.3.1.WT (FPF A.3.1.WT:4)](../../../sections/05-part-a---kernel-architecture-cluster/24-a-3-1-wt---design-and-interpret-a-method-work-trial.md#a31wt4---solution) supplies a formal method-trial contribution when the question requires it, distinguishing intended work, what occurred, conditions, burdens and evidence reach. Its result can feed the community inquiry; retain the evidence scope of any ordinary accounts used alongside it.

#### CB.10:4.5 - Decide what the evidence warrants

State the strongest claim supported at the relevant scope. A report establishes what a person says they experienced. A checked occurrence can establish the action and result observed there. A connected and corroborated account can show which resource a participant used and what the recipient could then do. If that account is used to explain why an outcome changed, examine its causal support.

Recover the causal question: the effect of an intervention, an explanation of an observed difference or a counterfactual. [FPF C.28](../../../sections/09-part-c---kernel-extension-specifications/54-c-28---causaluse-cal-causal-use-questions-identification-and.md) helps determine the supported statement, its limits and the next useful evidence question. [RMP.1 (RMP.1:4)](../../rmp/31-rmp-1-decide-whether-a-question-needs-research-and-when-to-s.md#rmp14---solution) first tests whether an adequate existing source answers that question and then whether the remaining inquiry warrants research. A persuasive story or a correlation between membership and performance does not provide missing identification.

Consider competing explanations that matter here. New participants, seasonal demand, another service, changed incentives or easier cases can alter the interpretation. Compare the conditions that could change the present explanation.

Keep the population claim within the evidence. A useful case can justify trying a repair without establishing how often the problem occurs. Several positive cases selected by an enthusiastic host still leave unsuccessful and nonresponding people outside the conclusion. Name a missing observation only when it affects reliance or the next decision.

Include burdens and distribution in the judgement. More answers can coexist with exhausted experts; a helpful resource can be inaccessible to newcomers. MA can supply a missing cost account, and HCD can supply a needed capability judgement. Do not collapse these unlike consequences into an overall “community health” number unless a warranted decision rule actually requires and supports that aggregation.

#### CB.10:4.6 - Change the condition that the inquiry exposes

Turn the account into a proposed action with a responsible receiver. A person who cannot find the first task may need a clearer entry through CB.3. A useful discussion that never reaches its intended work may need a recipient or application opportunity through CB.5. Missing time or access returns to CB.9; an inadequate practice returns to CB.14 or its actual method supplier.

Compare a change with a credible alternative, including retaining the current arrangement. Ask what useful result should appear, what burden it adds and what would make the change unsuitable. Provide the permissions, people and resources before treating the proposal as a commitment.

Try the change at the scale appropriate to the decision. Observe the particular use that prompted it and a consequential contrasting use when that distinction is still unresolved. Record what was changed and what actually happened; a simultaneous change elsewhere may prevent attributing a later difference to this one.

If the inquiry exposes a different purpose, return to CB.1 with the affected participants. If the arrangement no longer merits or can obtain support, CB.11 develops continuation, transfer, separation or ending. A decline in activity by itself does not choose among those responses.

#### CB.10:4.7 - Return the result and keep the learning loop useful

Return what was learned to those who supplied the experience and those who can act. Explain the supported benefit or failure, the limit on the conclusion and the next change. Use the detail needed by each receiver while preserving consequential uncertainty and permission for reuse.

Let participants use the account to improve their own practice. A failed application can become a correction to shared guidance; a successful adaptation can become a candidate for another context. CB.14 and Core's Method-development family qualify any wider method claim. A sponsor's summary can draw on that same grounded account without replacing it with an unsupported impact total.

Choose a next observation only when the question remains live. Follow an unfinished story when application becomes possible, or check the result of the chosen repair. Stop or simplify collection when it no longer affects decisions or learning. Preserve meaningful longitudinal comparisons when they are still used.

### CB.10:5 - Archetypal Grounding

#### CB.10:5.1 - Follow a useful exchange into a changed handover

In a constructed network of repair workshops, a sponsor asks whether monthly discussions justify protected staff time. Participants report useful conversations, but the current dashboard shows only attendance. The organiser selects a bounded inquiry: did the discussion help recipients act on difficult handovers, and what additional work did it require?

A participant recounts one exchange in which a colleague explained why the next workshop needed the actions already tried. The participant uses the shared handover form and adds those actions to a later handover. The receiving technician identifies the next inquiry without repeating that request. With permission, the organiser compares the account with the handover and the recipient's recorded first response. These observations support the claimed use and immediate result in that case.

A second participant copied the same handover form but filled its “prior actions” field with “usual checks.” The recipient still had to ask what had actually been done. A third participant obtained the explanation but has had no relevant case yet. The inquiry retains failed use and potential use alongside the first success.

The participants revise the prompt to request the actual actions and results and try it with the second workshop. Comparing preparation of two similar handovers, they observe that the revised account takes about two more minutes. Its recipient can select the next inquiry from the information supplied. The observed repair concerns handover information; equipment differences and other workshop changes still affect total repair time.

The organiser returns these cases, the preparation burden and the unresolved wider effect to participants and sponsor. They continue the protected discussion time for another limited period and observe repeated clarification requests for the relevant handover class. A later trend can challenge or extend the account; the present evidence does not establish that meetings caused a general reduction in repair time.

#### CB.10:5.2 - A quiet user and a useful practice

A person rarely posts in an association's forum but uses its public planning guide. An optional conversation recovers a particular plan they prepared and the parts of the guide they used. The receiving team confirms that the plan supplied the information needed for its next decision.

This is useful independent use with a local receiving result. The association keeps the guide accessible and improves its correction route.

#### CB.10:5.3 - More activity and a less useful answer

After a points programme begins, more short replies appear. Participants explain that any reply earns points. Recipients report that many replies repeat the question, and experienced contributors spend additional time correcting them. Comparing a few received answers with their follow-up requests confirms that visible response volume has risen without the hoped-for help in those cases.

The organisers use CB.6 to change what receives recognition and CB.5 to improve the answer-and-return practice. They compare the next answers for usefulness and correction burden. They retain the activity count as an observation, but it no longer stands for successful support.

#### CB.10:5.4 - An experience that does not need a productivity claim

A walking group exists partly for companionship. Members describe feeling welcome, name occasions when they could join a conversation and report that a changed meeting point excluded two regular participants. The organiser checks access with those participants and restores a reachable meeting arrangement.

If the group later promises a health effect, that new claim needs an appropriate evidence basis.

#### CB.10:5.5 - Sufficient evidence for an ordinary decision

A host needs a room for the next meeting. Confirmed attendance and the venue's capacity answer the booking question. The host uses them without starting a value investigation. If attendance remains high while people report that the meeting is unhelpful, that different decision reopens this pattern.

### CB.10:6 - Bias-Annotation

Positive stories are easier to elicit when the collector controls access, funding or recognition. Departed people, quiet users and unsuccessful applicants may remain invisible. Give criticism an appropriate receiving route, preserve nonresponse as a limit and seek a relevant contrary case. A numerical indicator can also favour what is easy to record; inspect whose work and experience its definition excludes.

### CB.10:7 - Conformance Checklist

For the intended decision, check that:

- The receiving decision, relevant people, period and claimed value are clear.
- Observations have meanings and populations suitable for that claim.
- Particular accounts recover the consequential connections from experience or resource to use and result, retaining missing links.
- Useful contrary observations, burdens and unsuccessful uses are included where they can change the judgement.
- Reports, observed occurrences, contribution explanations and causal claims retain their different evidence requirements.
- The proposed change has a receiver and the provision needed to try it.
- Participants receive a usable return, and continued collection has a reason.

A coherent local account can support a bounded improvement. A broader claim needs evidence with the corresponding reach.

### CB.10:8 - Common Anti-Patterns and How to Avoid Them

| Failure | Better move |
| --- | --- |
| Treat more posts or attendance as the promised benefit | Follow the activity into the experience or receiving work that matters. |
| Collect only promotional success stories | Recover specific use, conditions, cost and negative or incomplete outcomes. |
| Call an available guide an acquired capability | Obtain the relevant capability or use evidence, or retain the weaker access claim. |
| Attribute an outcome because the person belongs to the community | Recover the contribution and use a causal method when the causal difference is the question. |
| Explain a trend after the population or definition changed | Compare like observations or retain the change as a limit. |
| Make every participant file a complete value story after every event | Collect what can change the live decision and use voluntary, proportionate returns. |
| Measure indefinitely without changing anything | Make the receiving decision, revise the inquiry or stop collection that no longer helps. |

### CB.10:9 - Consequences

The organiser obtains a more useful account of contribution, failure and burden. Participants can learn from actual uses, supporters can judge a bounded continuation, and quiet independent use can remain visible without being converted into compulsory participation.

The method takes follow-up effort and can expose an unwelcome limit on an attractive claim. Some benefits remain uncertain or unobserved. Preserving that limit allows a proportionate decision while leaving the stronger unanswered question available for appropriate inquiry.

### CB.10:10 - Rationale

A contribution often matters through a sequence of changes in different places. Recovering the connections explains how the community could have helped; comparing them with other observations makes that explanation more answerable. Returning the account to practice turns evaluation into a useful contribution of its own.

Stories and indicators can correct each other's omissions. Neither makes causal identification automatic. Keeping the claim at its warranted scope also permits small practical repairs without requiring every community to become a research programme.

### CB.10:11 - SoTA-Echoing

The practical comparison is between activity reporting, a bounded inquiry that combines particular accounts with other observations, and a more demanding evaluation or causal study when its answer is needed.

### CB.10:12 - Relations

- **CB.1** distinguishes the cultural, participant and supporter results whose value is in question.
- **CB.3–CB.8, CB.12 and CB.13** provide entry, activity, recognition, organiser support, expectations, repetition and decision arrangements that an inquiry may improve.
- **CB.9** supplies resource and burden arrangements; **CB.11** uses the warranted result when choosing continuation or ending.
- **CB.14** receives useful adaptations and failures for qualification and maintenance of the repertoire; **CB.15 and CB.16** can supply contributions whose returns need following.
- **C.36** supplies cultural distinctions, **HCD** a needed capability judgement, and **MA** a needed resource or cost account.
- **A.3.1.WT** supplies formal trial evidence when selected; **C.28 and RMP** govern an unresolved causal or research question.
