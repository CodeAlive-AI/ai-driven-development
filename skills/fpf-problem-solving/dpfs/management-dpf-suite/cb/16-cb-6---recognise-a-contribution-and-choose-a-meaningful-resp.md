## CB.6 - Recognise a Contribution and Choose a Meaningful Response

> **Type:** Community-building method pattern
> **Status:** Stable

### CB.6:1 - Problem frame

Use this pattern when someone has made a useful contribution but cannot see what it helped, or when an organiser must choose how to acknowledge it. An answer quietly prevents wasted work. A person prepares the material that makes a discussion possible, while only the speaker receives credit. A public award attracts more visible activity but leaves the recipient uncomfortable.

The subject is recognition of a contribution: making its value intelligible to the contributor and choosing an appropriate response. This can be a specific thank-you, a returned account of use, agreed public credit, a useful introduction, access to an opportunity or a material benefit. A community-wide reward arrangement is one possible application; ordinary recognition need not become a programme.

**First useful result:** the contributor receives a truthful, understandable response to what they supplied, in a form they can accept, with any offered benefit or opportunity actually obtainable.

The organiser needs an account of the contribution and its use, or a recipient able to explain them. CB.5 supplies the return from receiving work. Where the result remains uncertain, recognition can concern a known contribution such as careful clarification without claiming an unobserved outcome.

**Ordinary non-use.** An adequate, welcomed response needs no additional award. If the problem is unavailable help, access, learning or compensation already owed, obtain that contribution directly. If nothing useful is known about the contribution yet, seek the relevant account before praising an imagined effect.

### CB.6:2 - Problem

Recognition can fail in opposite ways. Useful work disappears without a response, or an organiser attaches praise and prizes to whatever is easiest to count. The recipient may experience the same public gesture as appreciation, exposure or a demand to contribute more. A reward can also consume another person's time or imply an opportunity that nobody has agreed to provide.

How can an organiser recognise a real contribution, choose a response that matters to its recipient, and change the arrangement when it distorts the work or imposes an unwanted burden?

### CB.6:3 - Forces

| Force | Decision it changes |
| --- | --- |
| Timely acknowledgement and warranted claims | A contributor should not wait indefinitely for a response; the organiser must distinguish a known contribution from a later effect that is still unobserved. |
| Public credit and personal preference | Visibility can help a person, while privacy, shared authorship or an unwanted association can make another form preferable. |
| Consistent treatment and different contributions | Common criteria make an award understandable; comparable value cannot always be represented by a count of messages or hours. |
| Appreciation and control | A response can convey useful information and care, or make approval conditional on further unpaid effort. |
| A desirable opportunity and actual provision | An introduction, learning place or special access is useful only when the relevant people, conditions and capacity support it. |

### CB.6:4 - Solution

Recover what the person contributed and whom it helped. Choose the purpose and form of the response with their circumstances in view, make any promised benefit feasible, deliver it, and observe whether it served the person and the work.

**Working reminder:** identify the contribution; recover its use; choose a fitting response; supply what was offered; correct the response when its meaning or effects differ.

#### CB.6:4.1 - Find the useful contribution behind the visible activity

Start from an episode or result. Ask the person receiving the contribution what became easier, clearer or possible, and which part helped. A specific answer, a corrected explanation, preparation, translation, coordination or patient clarification can matter more than the most visible performance.

Use the evidence proportionate to the claim. A case owner's account can support “your clarification helped me identify the missing information”. A claim that the advice prevented a technical failure needs the relevant technical basis. When the later outcome is unknown, thank the person for the contribution you can identify and leave the later question open.

Recover joint work fairly. Ask who prepared the material, made access possible or performed a less visible part. Name the contributions in a way those people accept. Do not assign all credit to the person who presented the result merely because their work is easiest to see.

Distinguish useful effort from a successful result. A careful attempt can expose why a proposed approach will not work. Explain that value without relabelling the attempt as successful implementation. Conversely, extensive effort alone does not make incorrect advice fit for reuse.

For an ordinary exchange, this inquiry can be one question to the recipient. A scarce award or influential public endorsement needs a more inspectable basis because other people's opportunities depend on it.

#### CB.6:4.2 - Choose what the response should accomplish

Decide whether the present need is to make the contribution's effect visible, convey appreciation, give fair credit, offer a useful opportunity or change a recurring recognition arrangement. These aims can combine, but each should have an actual recipient and result.

Begin with the least elaborate response that fits. A private message explaining a concrete use may be more valuable than a generic public accolade. Returning a recipient's permitted account of what changed can give the contributor information that the organiser could not supply alone.

Find out how the person wants to be recognised when the choice matters. They may welcome a named example, prefer collective credit, want an introduction, or decline publicity. Existing preferences and the relationship may be sufficient for a routine thank-you; a new public profile, attribution of sensitive work or substantial opportunity warrants an explicit agreement.

People can contribute because they enjoy the activity, because they value its outcome, because they accept a responsibility or because provision is paid. A fitting response does not require the organiser to declare one motive authentic and the others defective. Ask whether the response respects the person's choice and helps them understand their contribution.

Keep recognition and an agreement for work clear. Pay or reimburse what was agreed. A gift does not settle an outstanding obligation, and a thank-you does not obtain a new commitment. If continued work needs funding, access or protected time, arrange those conditions through their owners.

#### CB.6:4.3 - Construct a response that can actually be received

The options below are examples.

| Response | How to make it useful | Condition that can change the choice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Specific acknowledgement and returned use | Name the contribution, explain a supported effect and let the recipient respond or correct the account. | The effect is unknown, the wording misattributes joint work, or the person prefers a different audience. |
| Agreed credit or public recognition | Describe what the person supplied, obtain the relevant consent and use an audience for whom the account has meaning. | Visibility exposes protected material, creates an unwanted association or overstates the person's qualification. |
| A useful connection | Establish why both parties might benefit, obtain their agreement and prepare the introduction around a real question. | The other person is unavailable, does not wish to participate or cannot supply the expected help. |
| Access to a useful activity or resource | State what is offered, obtain the provider's commitment and make the participation and selection conditions understandable. | The person lacks needed preparation, the resource is unavailable or the selection would remove access someone is already entitled to. |
| A material benefit | Choose something the person can use, explain any terms and provide it without an unannounced obligation. | Receipt creates expense, unsuitable conditions, disclosure or a burden greater than its value. |

A badge or title can carry an accurate statement, such as contribution to a particular resource. Make its meaning recoverable. If it is likely to be read as assessed professional competence or decision authority, either obtain that separate basis or change the description.

An invitation to speak can recognise useful experience and serve other participants, provided the person wants the work and has time to prepare it. Agree its scope and support. An invitation that becomes an unwanted unpaid assignment defeats the proposed benefit.

For an introduction or mentor opportunity, prepare the receiving question and agree the other person's contribution before promising access. CB.2 and CB.5 supply the exchange and contribution arrangements. A learning opportunity also needs an available learning provider and a suitable HCD contribution.

Check the receiving path for a material benefit. Can the person attend the event, use the resource or accept the item under their circumstances? Offer a reasonable alternative when the original benefit is unsuitable.

#### CB.6:4.4 - Make a recurring arrangement understandable and revisable

Use a recurring arrangement when contributions are repeatedly missed or a shared award needs consistent treatment. Begin with the useful work being recognised and the reason the arrangement is needed. Retain ordinary personal acknowledgement alongside it.

Explain how a contribution becomes known, who examines it and what the recognition means. Use examples of different qualifying contributions so that preparation, correction and quiet help can be noticed. Choose an observation window that fits the work: immediate message counts cannot reveal a result that appears only after later use.

If nominations or peer responses are used, ask for a concrete account of the contribution and its value. Popularity can help locate a candidate contribution, but it does not supply its correctness or receiving effect. Where a decision-maker has a relevant interest, use another suitable judgement or make that interest visible to the people relying on the award.

Make the burden proportionate. A small voluntary group may use a brief account at an existing meeting; a scarce professional opportunity needs clearer criteria and a way to correct an error.

Examine what the arrangement makes attractive to produce. If a count rewards short duplicate replies, replace or remove that count and inspect the useful response. If a ranking conceals collaborative work, recognise the joint contribution or its distinct parts. A peer vote alone does not solve these failures; it can favour well-known people or visible work too.

Provide a practical correction route. A contributor should be able to point out mistaken attribution, decline publicity or question a factual claim. Correct the account and affected benefit where necessary. When changing an established reward, explain the reason and honour commitments already made; do not quietly change the meaning of an earned acknowledgement.

#### CB.6:4.5 - Deliver the response and inspect its meaning

Deliver the recognition close enough to the contribution for the relation to be clear. Use ordinary language: what the person did, why it mattered and who benefited, within the available evidence. Avoid generic superlatives when they obscure that information.

A response can be offered without requiring a grateful public reply. Give the person a usable way to correct the account, change attribution or decline the opportunity. Keep a refusal compatible with continued ordinary participation.

Where the response could affect future participation, ask how it was received. Did the person understand what was valued? Was the opportunity useful? Did the gesture feel like appreciation or pressure to maintain an unwanted role? The organiser's good intention cannot answer these questions for the recipient.

Observe the work as well as the visible reaction. An increase in posts may coexist with less useful help. Continued contribution may reflect an accepted responsibility, a valued outcome or pressure. Use the person's account and relevant receiving results to interpret the change; silence or a click alone leaves that question open.

When the response creates a problem, repair its specific cause. Change the audience, clarify a claim, supply a promised opportunity, remove an unsuitable incentive or revise the expected commitment. Do not try to compensate for absent help with more praise.

#### CB.6:4.6 - Keep recognition distinct from continuing participation

A well-received acknowledgement closes the present recognition question. It need not establish a future obligation or a habit of returning.

Use CB.12 when the person wants help making a useful action easier to repeat. Use CB.3 for a changed role or a reduced commitment, and CB.5 when the needed repair is an absent contribution or receiving response. Learning feedback and capability assessment use their own HCD criteria.

Keep only the account needed for attribution, the offered benefit or later use. Publicly accumulating every acknowledgement can create a ranking that nobody intended to introduce. Retain a simple response when it does the job.

### CB.6:5 - Archetypal Grounding

#### CB.6:5.1 - A useful correction receives a specific return

In a constructed repairer association, one participant corrects a misleading applicability example in a shared reference. Another person confirms that the correction helped them distinguish two equipment configurations. The organiser asks permission to return that account.

The contributor prefers a private message and agrees to a brief credit on the corrected page. The response names the distinction they clarified and the use that the recipient reported.

A later public summary credits both the person who found the problem and the maintainer who checked and incorporated the correction. If a use report contradicts the correction, the subject review reopens; the acknowledgement of the contributed work does not settle the technical question.

#### CB.6:5.2 - A leaderboard rewards the wrong work

An online support group awards points for every answer. Several participants begin splitting replies and repeating material. A quiet contributor who prepares a reliable explanation receives little attention.

The organiser inspects actual cases with their recipients. Some quick replies are useful, but the count does not distinguish them from duplication. The group ends the per-message competition, explains the change and retains already-earned acknowledgements with their original limited meaning. Useful case returns and corrections can receive specific recognition without a new public ranking.

If a later formal award is wanted, it uses an account of a contribution and a suitable judgement. Popular nominations are considered alongside the receiving use and overlooked preparatory work.

#### CB.6:5.3 - A mentor invitation cannot promise another person's time

An organiser wants to thank a volunteer by offering a meeting with an experienced practitioner. Before announcing it, they learn what the volunteer would like to discuss and ask the practitioner whether they can help.

The practitioner can offer one bounded conversation but no continuing mentorship. The volunteer accepts that scope, receives preparation guidance and has a useful exchange.

In a changed case, the volunteer has no use for the introduction and prefers access to an existing reference resource. Recognition changes form without reducing their standing in the group.

#### CB.6:5.4 - Appreciation does not settle provision

A garden association publicly thanks someone who has repeatedly prepared shared equipment. The person explains that the work now exceeds the time they can offer. More praise would make the burden more visible without removing it.

The association keeps the acknowledgement, agrees a smaller voluntary contribution and arranges the remaining maintenance through a responsible provider. Any payment already agreed is settled separately.

### CB.6:6 - Bias-Annotation

Visible speakers, prolific writers and people close to the organiser are easier to notice. Look through the receiving work for less visible preparation, correction and coordination. An organiser can also project their own preferred reward onto another person; inspect the recipient's circumstances and response.

### CB.6:7 - Conformance Checklist

A fitting response establishes:

- the actual contribution and the supported account of its value;
- the purpose and audience of recognition;
- a form the person can accept;
- feasible provision of any offered benefit;
- proportionate criteria and correction where opportunities depend on them;
- a way to change an unwanted, misleading or distorting response.

An ordinary thank-you can rely on a known episode. A public competence claim, scarce award or promised service needs the corresponding assessment, decision or provision.

### CB.6:8 - Common Anti-Patterns and How to Avoid Them

| Wrong turn | Practical repair |
| --- | --- |
| Praise activity without finding its useful contribution | Obtain the relevant receiving account and name what it supports. |
| Credit only the person who presented joint work | Recover preparation and other contributions and agree the attribution. |
| Make every acknowledgement public | Choose the audience with the person's circumstances and preferences in view. |
| Promise an introduction or learning place without its provider | Obtain a bounded commitment and feasible access first. |
| Treat popularity or message volume as contribution quality | Inspect the contribution and its use; revise the misleading incentive. |
| Turn recognition into a new unpaid obligation | Agree new work separately and respect a refusal. |
| Replace owed payment or missing support with appreciation | Fulfil the actual obligation or obtain the missing provision. |

### CB.6:9 - Consequences

The contributor can understand what their work helped, and the organiser can offer a response that has real meaning and feasible terms. Less visible contributions become easier to notice. Recognition can remain small and personal.

Finding the receiving effect and providing an opportunity consume effort. A recurring award also requires criteria, correction and capacity. When those costs add little value, a truthful direct response is preferable.

### CB.6:10 - Rationale

Recognition connects a contribution with a person's understanding of its value and of their relation to others. The same gesture can convey different meanings because its audience, terms and the person's circumstances differ. Those conditions provide reasons for choosing a response; the recipient's account helps establish how it was received.

An incentive also changes what is worth producing under its rules. A measure that is easy to increase can draw effort away from the receiving need. Inspecting the work and the recipient's experience makes that displacement visible and gives the organiser a specific repair.

### CB.6:11 - SoTA-Echoing

| Source line or developed treatment | Contribution used | Adaptation and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spinks, The Business of Belonging (2021), physical PDF 119–129 | Return the experienced value of contribution and consider different forms of acknowledgement, status, connection, access and benefits. | Treat these as options. Claims that payment always displaces care, that every community needs status growth or that a peer reward system prevents gaming are too broad for choosing the response. |
| Self-Determination Theory, especially CET and OIT | Distinguish interest in an activity, instrumental motivation, internalisation and the perceived autonomy or control of social responses. | These distinctions guide questions about the arrangement; they do not predict one person's reaction to a particular award without evidence. |
| CB.5 and LDR.1:4.4 | Return a contribution's actual result and conduct a task-focused conversation about what helped and what needs changing. | Appreciation has its own purpose; technical acceptance, developmental feedback and an organisational decision retain their criteria. |

Compared with an unexamined prize or leaderboard, this construction requires more attention to the contribution and recipient but can reveal misleading attribution, unsuitable terms or an absent provider. The simpler alternative is often a specific acknowledgement.

Source returns: [Spinks](https://www.wiley-vch.de/en/areas-interest/finance-economics-law/the-business-of-belonging-978-1-119-76612-4); [the official SDT account, including CET and OIT](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/theory/). Reconsider the response when the contribution, its receiving effect, the person's preferences or the promised opportunity changes.

### CB.6:12 - Relations

- **Uses CB.5:** an account of the contribution and its returned use.
- **Uses CB.2 and CB.4:** a useful connection or shared activity when offered as recognition.
- **Uses CB.3:** a changed responsibility, support condition or reduced voluntary commitment.
- **Uses [LDR.1 (LDR.1:4.4)](../ldr/08-ldr-1---obtain-and-continue-the-leadership-contribution-need.md#ldr144---perform-the-smallest-sufficient-working-conversation):** a task-focused conversation and return when a concrete working contribution is needed.
- **Uses [HCD](../../human-capability-engineering-dpf-suite/hcd/_index.md):** the learning contribution or assessment actually promised by an opportunity or qualification claim.
- **Combines with CB.12:** recognition may accompany useful repetition, while the repeatable action and its conditions require their own construction.
