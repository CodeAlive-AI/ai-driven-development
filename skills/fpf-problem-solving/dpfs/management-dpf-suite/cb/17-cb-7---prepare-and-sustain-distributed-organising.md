## CB.7 - Prepare and Sustain Distributed Organising

> **Type:** Community-building method pattern
> **Status:** Stable

### CB.7:1 - Problem frame

Use this pattern when useful participation depends on one organiser, when capable organisers cannot attend to several emerging needs at once, when a local group needs people who can sustain its work, or when an invitation through the most visible members fails to reach the people concerned. A founder still receives every request. A willing host has a title but cannot obtain a room or help with a difficult meeting. A quiet practitioner is the person others actually trust to explain a change.

The governed work is organising participation through capable, willing people and usable relationships. It includes finding those people, preparing a bounded contribution, enabling temporary groups of participants to support the activity, and arranging continuation or handover. Some organisers hold formal positions; others help without one. The source and scope of any authority to decide for others must be established separately.

**First useful result:** a willing person or temporary participant group can perform an agreed organising contribution at the next relevant occasion, with the access, preparation, time, help and decision boundaries it requires. Where continuity matters, another feasible performer or an honest reduced promise is also available.

Start with the useful activity and the contribution currently missing. A general ambition to "develop leaders" is insufficient to choose whom to involve or what to teach. CB.5 can identify the receiving contribution; LDR.1 develops the explanation, working conversation or preparation that enables it.

**Ordinary non-use:** a stable small exchange already has sufficient willing support and a workable absence arrangement. Continue it without creating a leadership programme. If the intended result is wider use of a practice among independent people, CB.1 may instead select an explanation or learning provision. Shared decision rights that are absent or contested require CB.13; assigning more organisers alone will not establish them.

### CB.7:2 - Problem

An organiser's visible work can conceal the relationships and conditions that make participation possible. Copying the role title leaves those conditions behind. Selecting only frequent speakers can omit competent helpers, people trusted by a less visible group and people who perform essential preparation.

Delegation also fails when the initiator continues to settle every exception, or gives away work while retaining every useful decision. The apparent replacement cannot act, participants return to the founder, and a volunteer absorbs an indefinite burden.

A capable host can also miss simultaneous difficulties: some participants have no usable way to contribute, a discussion consumes the time needed for another question, and a recurring practical problem goes unnoticed. Training the host may help a capability gap. A shortage of attention can instead call for participants to share a bounded observation and support task.

The practical problem is to obtain the needed contribution and its continuation while preserving local knowledge, willing participation and an effective way to handle limits.

### CB.7:3 - Forces

| Force | Working consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Reach and trust differ. | A widely known speaker may not be the person a particular group consults about its work. |
| Willingness and preparation differ. | Interest makes an invitation possible; representative work reveals what preparation remains. |
| Local freedom and shared commitments interact. | Local organisers need room to adapt, while participants must be able to rely on the common promises. |
| Continuity takes spare capacity. | A nominal substitute who has no time or access cannot cover an absence. |
| Attention and conference time are limited. | A support group needs a bounded task and time to interpret its observations together; adding duties can consume the participation it should enable. |
| Evidence about relationships is partial. | Missing nominations or messages may reflect the inquiry's boundary, not isolation or lack of contribution. |
| Support can become dependence. | Keep useful assistance while making it possible to perform without the initiator doing the work. |

### CB.7:4 - Solution

Construct the next organising contribution around the people who can actually supply and receive it. Investigate relationships only far enough to choose a useful action; prepare performance around a representative episode; then arrange a sustainable next occasion.

#### CB.7:4.1 - Name the contribution and the failure it should remedy

Describe what participants need to receive. For example, a local session needs an attainable place and a prepared host; new members need an answer from someone who can help; a reference needs corrections returned to its maintainer. Recover what currently happens, including work performed before and after the visible event.

Identify which part depends on a particular person and why. They may hold the only key, know the relevant specialist, settle a disputed responsibility or supply the explanation nobody else can give. These are different replacement problems. Reassigning the meeting chair will not replace an unavailable specialist. When capable organisers cannot notice or support several aspects of the activity at once, consider the variant for temporary participant groups in CB.7:4.7.

Make the commitment small enough to understand and large enough to complete. Agree the intended result, next occasion, expected effort, relevant conditions, available support and a point at which the person can reconsider. Name any preparation, follow-up or emotionally demanding incident work; an hour hosting a meeting may require several hours beyond the meeting itself.

Separate the work from the authority it needs. A volunteer may arrange an event within an existing offer but need a venue owner to approve access and a community decision-maker to change membership conditions. Use the effective arrangements supplied by CB.13 or the relevant organisation. OCE.6 can obtain an assignment and enabling contributions where those need to be established.

#### CB.7:4.2 - Find the actual paths of help, explanation and influence

When the relevant people are already known through recent work, approach them directly. When reach or understanding is uncertain, start with the question the relationship evidence must answer. "Who helps new repairers judge an unfamiliar case?" selects different relationships from "Who can book the room?" or "Whose explanation do members of this local group consult?"

Ask people about concrete episodes and whom they approached. Include participants outside the most visible circle and an accessible way to answer privately. Compare their accounts with permitted observations or work traces. Arrange a useful introduction with the introduced person's agreement; leave the connection unresolved when the available accounts are insufficient.

Keep the different relations intelligible. Being named frequently, connecting otherwise separated groups, being trusted on a subject and holding a mandate can belong to different people. OCE.2 supplies recovery of actual work, authority and resource relations when formal descriptions are misleading. A simple account of who supplied which help may be enough.

Use the inquiry to choose an action:

| What the inquiry reveals | Possible next move |
| --- | --- |
| A group already obtains reliable help from a willing local practitioner. | Ask whether that person wants a bounded organising contribution and what support it needs. |
| Two groups have useful knowledge but no workable exchange. | With their agreement, obtain an introduction or a joint case through CB.2 or CB.5, according to the contribution needed. |
| The central organiser hears only one group's account. | Obtain the missing group's own account before adapting the offer. |
| Several people rely on one overburdened intermediary. | Reduce the promise, prepare another contributor or provide an additional route that participants can actually use. |
| People understand the proposal but find the practice unsuitable. | Return the substantive problem to its owner; changing the messenger will not repair the practice. |

With a willing participant who understands the receiving group's work, prepare the explanation of the proposed change. Start from a familiar case: what could people do differently, what useful result is expected, and which conditions or commitments would change? Use language and examples the recipients can inspect. Return a substantive objection to the person who can change the practice or offer.

Ask a recipient to work through that case and explain what they would do next and where they would seek help. If the action or consequence is misunderstood, repair the explanation and try the affected part again. If it is understood but infeasible or unwanted, return that condition instead of repeating the message. Later use can show whether the explanation was sufficient for the actual circumstances; agreement with the organiser is not the test of understanding.

Explain why information about relationships is being obtained, who will see it and what decision it will inform. Obtain only what that use needs. A public network drawing can reveal relationships that people did not agree to disclose; use a restricted account or an aggregate explanation when that is sufficient. If participants use a shared drawing to reconsider their cooperation, obtain agreement to that activity as well.

Distinguish evidence used to select an organiser from changes to the relationships themselves. Later, inspect whether the promised exchange occurs and supplies value. Increased connectivity or similar behaviour among connected people alone cannot establish that the organising intervention caused improvement.

#### CB.7:4.3 - Invite and select for the actual work

Make an individual, intelligible offer: the contribution, its recipient, available freedom, expected burden, support and way to leave or reduce it. Ask what the person wants from the work and which conditions would make it feasible. Declining the role can leave their existing participation valuable.

Use evidence appropriate to the contribution. A potential host can work through a representative session; a prospective moderator can explain how they would respond to a difficult case; a maintenance organiser can show how they obtain and return a correction. Assess the needed work, rather than using visible enthusiasm or friendship as its proxy.

Select preparation and support from the gaps revealed. One person may need the local access procedure, another practice in giving a brief, and another a qualified colleague for a domain judgement. A person who can welcome members need not be able to teach the practice or decide a complaint. Obtain those separate contributions where needed.

Make selection understandable when several people seek a role. State the work criteria, who selects, how relevant conflicts are handled and how an unsuccessful applicant can obtain a useful explanation. Keep the inquiry proportionate: a small voluntary hosting task may need one working conversation, while a role with consequential decisions requires the relevant mandate and preparation.

Recognise the contribution through CB.6 in a form the person values. Distinguish that acknowledgement from compensation, expenses, access and time needed to make the work possible. If the community needs dependable service beyond willing voluntary capacity, change the provision or promise.

#### CB.7:4.4 - Prepare the first episode and make support obtainable

Use LDR.1's brief, practice and debrief around the actual next contribution. Explain the purpose and demonstrate the difficult part where that helps. Let the new organiser explain their own next actions and practise a representative difficulty. Return concrete feedback; if a capability must be learned or assessed, obtain the corresponding HCD contribution.

Make the working guidance useful at the point of action. A local host may need a room contact, an accessible invitation example, the session's purpose and shape, and a response to an interruption. Put a frequent action near its cue and a consequential exception near the decision it changes.


Obtain the actual conditions: permission and access, material, time, a person who will answer a difficult question and a feasible response window. A name in a contact list is insufficient if that person is unavailable. Agree what the organiser should pause or reduce while help is absent.

Arrange peer support where organisers have recurring related problems. Use a feasible rhythm for exchanging cases and working examples that help with the next contribution. Keep sensitive incidents within the agreed handling route.

After the first episode, inspect the receiving result and the organiser's work. What could participants do? What required help? Which commitment took more effort than expected? Use the account to repair the specific condition. A completed training session alone does not show that the organising contribution can now be supplied.

#### CB.7:4.5 - Give local organisers usable freedom

For a local group or chapter, agree which shared commitments it carries and which choices it can make locally. Common commitments might concern a promised service, use of a shared name, access to a shared resource or a rule for reporting incidents. Local choice might concern timing, examples or a meeting format. Select these boundaries from the actual common work.

Give each retained central decision a reachable decision-maker and a response time compatible with local work. Give local decisions the means to take effect. An organiser who may choose a meeting time but cannot obtain the venue does not yet have a usable arrangement.

Work through an ordinary variation and a consequential exception. Can the host change a format when attendance is small? Who handles an event that would exceed the agreed budget or exclude a group previously promised access? What happens if two chapters want the same resource? Return unresolved decision rights to CB.13 and resource conditions to their owner or ECO.7.

Let local observations change the shared offer where warranted. Ask organisers what their participants could not use and bring back the outcome of that request. Otherwise local autonomy can become permission to absorb the failures of an inflexible central promise.

#### CB.7:4.6 - Continue, hand over or reduce the promise

Review the next contribution while there is still time to change it. Check willingness, effort and support as well as completion. If work repeatedly exceeds the accepted commitment, reduce the activity, obtain more provision or change the arrangement with its recipient.

Prepare a successor through real work. Introduce the relevant people, transfer permitted working material and obtain their own access. Explain unresolved commitments and consequential exceptions. Agree when responsibility changes and tell the recipients whom to approach.

Where independence from the initiator matters, let another prepared person perform an episode without the initiator doing it for them. Preserve the domain assistance, accessibility support and decision routes that the work legitimately requires. Inspect what the new organiser obtained, where they still needed help and what prevented completion.

A willing, capable organiser may continue under the applicable appointment arrangement; another role may need rotation to share learning or burden. An expiring mandate still needs its proper renewal. CB.13 supplies that decision, while this pattern prepares someone to perform the resulting contribution.

If no feasible successor or support can be obtained, inform participants of the reduced or ending offer. Settle outstanding commitments, provide a usable route for remaining questions and preserve material under its applicable permissions. A declared handover is complete only when the recipient can take up the agreed work; otherwise return the missing condition or end that promise explicitly.

#### CB.7:4.7 - Let temporary participant groups support the activity

Select this variant when sharing attention or follow-up among participants could repair a specific difficulty. A small group might attend to opportunities to contribute, the usefulness of the agenda and time allocation, or what happens when people use a shared aid afterward. Choose the contribution that is missing. An adequate host, one helper or an existing maintenance arrangement may already supply it.

Invite willing participants to one bounded task for a meeting or an agreed period. Explain what they should help the community obtain, the effort and access it needs, and when the task ends. People may continue their ordinary participation without joining a support group. In a small community, combine compatible tasks or select fewer; one person need not carry several simultaneous observation duties.

Agree the group's scope with the people who can authorise it. Distinguish an action it may take during the activity from a suggestion requiring someone else's decision. A group may offer an already permitted way to submit questions, signal an agreed time limit or maintain an authorised summary. Changing membership conditions or settling a complaint still needs its proper decision route. Use CB.13 when the required mandate does not exist.

Provide time for members to confer before, during or just after the activity, or at a feasible interval for a longer task. Include that time when designing the activity through [CB.4](14-cb-4---design-a-space-and-shared-activity-for-its-purpose.md). Agree how they will obtain the relevant observations without collecting unnecessary personal information. Silence can mean wanted quiet participation; ask about an apparent obstacle before treating it as exclusion. Incident details belong in CB.8's handling route.

At the agreed occasion, compare what members noticed and what it could mean for their task. Select a feasible response, act within the agreed scope and observe what the response enables. Where the change needs another person's decision, return a concrete proposal to that person and obtain a response. A group with no time to confer has not supplied this shared interpretation merely by collecting individual notes.

Return what happened to the participants or organisers who can use it. A proposed change to the next agenda or participation format goes to CB.4's activity construction; a question about what the activity helped people do goes to [CB.10](20-cb-10---understand-the-value-of-participation-and-cultural-w.md). Keep an observation, its interpretation and a claim of benefit distinct. For example, a newly used question route shows access to that exchange; whether it changed later practice needs the recipient's account and appropriate further observations.

Close the temporary assignment at its agreed limit. Settle unfinished suggestions with a willing receiver, retain only permitted useful material, and decide whether a later occasion needs the task again. If so, agree its burden and performers anew or arrange the handover in CB.7:4.6. Reduce or stop it when the observation or conference burden prevents useful participation or nobody can receive its result.

### CB.7:5 - Archetypal Grounding

#### CB.7:5.1 - A local explanation reveals the missing condition

In a constructed repairer association, the main meeting host invites members to return tested corrections to a common reference. The invitation reaches regular speakers, but a smaller weekend group rarely responds. The organiser initially suspects that the message needs more emphasis.

They ask people in both groups whom they consult after a difficult repair. Members of the weekend group name a practitioner who rarely attends the main meeting. With that person's agreement, the organiser obtains a conversation about recent cases. The group already understands the request; its members cannot prepare the confidential customer details expected by the current submission form.

The practitioner helps test a permitted account that retains the configuration and observed result while removing unnecessary customer information. A maintainer confirms that this is sufficient for the selected class of correction. One weekend repairer submits such an account and receives a useful return.

The newly found connection helped recover the obstacle. The repair was to the contribution conditions, not simply the volume of promotion. The practitioner agrees to introduce the new route once; they do not accept an indefinite duty to collect every case. The maintainer and organiser provide the continuing route.

#### CB.7:5.2 - Another host carries the next meeting

A garden group relies on its founder to open the room, welcome participants, explain the day's shared work and settle every uncertainty. A volunteer offers to host the next meeting.

They identify two replaceable contributions: room access and the preparation brief. A venue steward supplies authorised access; an experienced gardener remains available for unfamiliar growing questions. The volunteer observes one brief, rehearses the next task with feedback and agrees what to do if attendance or conditions change.

At the next meeting, the founder is absent. The volunteer opens the room, gives the brief and adapts the grouping to the people present. A plant-health question goes to the experienced gardener. The selected hosting contribution works without the founder while subject expertise remains available.

The debrief reveals that returning equipment takes longer than agreed. The group obtains a separate helper for that task. If no helper is available, it reduces the equipment-dependent activity rather than silently extending the host's commitment.

#### CB.7:5.3 - A local chapter reaches the limit of its freedom

A professional chapter may choose the timing and examples for an agreed case session. It may also use a shared name under stated conditions. Its host wants to turn the session into a paid service with a new guarantee.

The host can propose that change but cannot establish the guarantee or spend the central association's funds. The relevant decision-makers consider the new commitment under CB.13; the host continues the already supported session while that question is open.

#### CB.7:5.4 - A small exchange needs no leadership programme

Three neighbours already take turns preparing a monthly materials exchange. Each knows the bounded task, one can cover an absence, and unused materials have a clear destination. A proposal to create a leadership ladder would add selection and reporting work without improving the exchange. They retain the arrangement and revisit it only if the work or conditions change.

#### CB.7:5.5 - A capable host cannot notice everything at once

In another constructed repairer session, eighteen people have ninety minutes to compare difficult cases. The host and a successor can both facilitate, but the previous session left an emerging question unheard while one account consumed most of the discussion. Further host training is one option; the group first tries sharing two bounded attention tasks.

Four participants volunteer in two pairs for this session. One pair attends to usable opportunities to contribute. The other attends to time and questions that the agenda is failing to serve. Their scope permits offering the existing written-question route and signalling the agreed time limits. The host retains decisions about changing the agenda. Each pair has five minutes together during a planned pause and ten minutes just after the session; the volunteers accept this burden for one occasion.

One member of the first pair notices that three speakers have taken every turn; the other notices an unused written-question route. During the pause, they compare these observations and ask a quiet newcomer whether the available routes work for them. The newcomer wants to ask a question without interrupting the case. A written question reaches the host, who gives it a place in the remaining discussion. The second pair identifies two related questions that cannot both receive a useful treatment in the remaining time. It proposes a focused next session; the host accepts that proposal through CB.4 and a participant agrees to prepare the shared material.

At the debrief, the first pair reports that the newcomer received a relevant reference. The second pair confirms the next-session preparation with its willing receiver. Both temporary assignments end; the existing organiser and successor remain responsible for their own agreed contributions. Through CB.10, the organiser later obtains the recipient's account: the reference supplied the previously missing condition needed to select a diagnostic check.

The next event is shortened to thirty minutes, and only one participant offers additional help. There is no feasible pair or conference time. The organiser retires the group variant for that event, asks the willing helper to collect unanswered questions for the final three minutes, and defers the wider participation inquiry.

### CB.7:6 - Bias-Annotation

Visible participation is easier to notice than quiet preparation, private help or work by people absent from central meetings. Relationship inquiry can also reproduce the organiser's existing circle. Compare concrete episodes from the affected groups, retain uncertainty about missing connections and allow people to decline disclosure or a proposed role. Company chapter models bring useful support practices but do not make unpaid work or brand growth the purpose of every community.

### CB.7:7 - Conformance Checklist

For an ordinary next contribution, ask:

- What useful result will participants receive, and which organising work is missing?
- Why is the proposed person suitable and willing, and what preparation remains?
- Are their authority, access, time and help actually sufficient for the next occasion?
- Which choices are local, and where does an unresolved exception go?
- What did a performed episode reveal about value, burden and needed support?
- If a temporary participant group is used, can it confer, act within its scope and return a useful response before its assignment ends?
- If continuation matters, can the next person perform the work, or has the promise been reduced honestly?

A stronger claim about professional competence, influence or cultural continuation requires evidence appropriate to that claim. The ordinary result here is the obtained organising contribution and its supported next use.

### CB.7:8 - Common Anti-Patterns and How to Avoid Them

| Misuse | Repair |
| --- | --- |
| Appoint the most visible member and assume they reach everyone. | Recover the relationships relevant to the missing contribution and include the affected group's account. |
| Give a title without access or a usable decision boundary. | Obtain the specific enabling condition and establish the decision right through its proper source. |
| Treat an enthusiastic volunteer as permanently available. | Agree the burden and review point; change the promise when provision becomes insufficient. |
| Supply a large playbook while the difficult action remains unexplained. | Develop that action around a representative episode and feedback. |
| Let the founder rescue every trial of a successor. | Let the prepared successor perform while retaining the support the role actually needs. |
| Centralise every exception under the name of consistency. | State the common commitment and give local organisers the choices and means compatible with it. |
| Give every participant several observation duties and no time to confer. | Select fewer voluntary tasks, provide conference time and a receiving decision, or use a smaller individual contribution. |
| Interpret an unsuitable practice as a communication failure. | Return the substantive defect to the practice or offer owner. |

### CB.7:9 - Consequences

Participants obtain organising help through a broader and more understandable arrangement. The community can preserve useful local knowledge, change an unrealistic promise and continue a selected activity when the initiator is absent.

Preparation and spare capacity cost effort. Relationship inquiry and shared working guidance also need maintenance when people or conditions change. Use them where they alter the next contribution; stable small arrangements can remain simple. Temporary support groups also spend participants' attention and conference time. Their continued use depends on what those contributions enable, not on keeping the groups in existence.

### CB.7:10 - Rationale

Organising is effective when someone can obtain the contributions that let others participate. Those contributions depend on specific relationships and conditions. Recovering them explains why a nominal replacement or a generic leadership course can fail.

A representative episode links preparation to the receiving work. A later episode performed by another person tests the continuity actually claimed. Keeping authority and expertise separate makes it possible to distribute useful work without inventing powers or demanding that one organiser know everything. Temporary participant groups add a different contribution: several people notice, interpret and support an aspect of the activity together. Their return can change the next activity even when the host already knows how to facilitate it.

### CB.7:11 - SoTA-Echoing

The practice question is how to obtain distributed organising when relationships, local conditions and continuity matter. The useful line combines inquiry into actual work with a bounded offer, situated preparation, usable local freedom and observation of the next contribution. Where concurrent attention is missing, a temporary participant group can add shared observation, conference, permitted intervention and a received proposal. It accepts the cost of preparation and support in place of relying on visible enthusiasm or a founder's continuing rescue.
