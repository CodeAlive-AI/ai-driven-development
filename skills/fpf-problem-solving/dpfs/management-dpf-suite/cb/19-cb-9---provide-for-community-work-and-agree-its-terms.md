## CB.9 - Provide for Community Work and Agree Its Terms

> **Type:** Community-building method pattern
> **Status:** Stable

### CB.9:1 - Problem frame

Use this pattern when a useful community activity depends on contributions that have not been secured, when organisers are carrying work beyond what they agreed, or when a sponsor's conditions change what participants can expect. A room is paid for but nobody can prepare the session; several departments expect different results from one organiser; a voluntary contribution has become a service with fixed deadlines.

The subject is the arrangement that provides labour, money, access and other resources for cultural or community work. The move is to connect a bounded promise to willing contributions, effective authority and terms that the parties can sustain. The same provision construction can support a community's contribution to independent carriers: maintaining a public guide can need this arrangement even when its users never meet. Their cultural continuation does not thereby become a community-participation process.

The first useful result is an offer that can be delivered for a stated period: what will be provided, by whom, under which conditions, what participants and supporters receive, and what will change if support falls short. A smaller activity, a paid service, a different source of support or a decision to end an unsupported promise can be the result.

The intended user organises the activity and can obtain decisions from resource owners and affected contributors. The pattern uses accounting and operating results where calculation or scheduling needs specialist work.

**Ordinary non-use.** An already authorised, adequately provided activity can use its existing arrangements. If the remaining question is the value of the activity, use CB.10. If nobody can yet explain what contribution is worth supporting, return to CB.1 before seeking a larger budget.

### CB.9:2 - Problem

Community work consumes resources even when participants do not pay. Invitation, preparation, moderation, responding to difficult cases, maintaining material and handing over to a successor can remain hidden behind a successful meeting. Organisers may conceal that deficit by donating more time until an absence exposes it.

Support also has conditions. A sponsor may expect recruitment while participants expect confidential help. A platform provider can control access to material that the group depends on. Several beneficiaries can each assume another party is paying. Increasing income alone leaves these conflicting promises unresolved.

How can the organiser provide the useful work, agree who bears its burdens and benefits, and revise the promise before a shortfall becomes an involuntary obligation?

### CB.9:3 - Forces

| Force | Decision it changes |
| --- | --- |
| Useful promise and feasible supply | A more generous offer can help participants while exceeding the available capability or time. |
| Voluntary contribution and dependable service | People can willingly help within limits; a service promise may require firmer provision and compensation. |
| Several beneficiaries | Participants, employers, sponsors and independent users can gain different things and accept different costs. |
| Visibility and hidden work | Public sessions are easy to count; preparation, response and maintenance can determine whether they continue. |
| Resource ownership and community judgement | A funding decision can enable work without granting its owner every decision about participation or content. |
| Immediate support and future dependence | A convenient grant or platform can create a later renewal, withdrawal or transfer problem. |

### CB.9:4 - Solution

Build the arrangement from the promised activity and its resource dependencies. Compare support options with the affected people, obtain the actual commitments and test an ordinary use together with a plausible shortfall.

**Working reminder:** bound the promise; recover the work; compare support and terms; obtain commitments; try delivery; revise before the burden becomes hidden.

#### CB.9:4.1 - Bound the work that support is supposed to enable

State the receiving result and period. “Keep the community active” is too broad to show what must be provided. “Offer one monthly case discussion with a qualified facilitator and return the agreed resources” exposes a different demand from “answer every member's question within one working day.”

Recover each material expectation with the party that holds it. Participants may need dependable access; an employer may permit staff time to develop practice; a sponsor may want evidence of useful reach. Ask what each party offers, needs and has authority to decide. A staff member's enthusiasm does not allocate their employer's time.

Resolve incompatible promises before costing them as one programme. A confidential peer conversation and an identifiable sales-lead report require different permissions and participant expectations. Change the proposed terms, separate the activities or decline one promise when the conflict remains. CB.1 helps reconsider the contribution; CB.13 supplies a missing collective decision mandate.

For a group with several supporters, identify the person who can reconcile the demands on the same organiser. Retain the distinct results and receiving parties. Adding every possible sponsor benefit to one activity can multiply preparation, data collection and reporting beyond the activity's useful work.

#### CB.9:4.2 - Recover the resource demand, including quiet work

Walk through an actual or proposed occurrence with those who will perform it. Recover preparation, delivery and the follow-up needed for its result. Include access administration, conflict response and material maintenance where the promise depends on them. Ask what happens during absence or an unusually difficult case.

Express consequential requirements in usable units and time windows. Qualified facilitator-hours, a room booking and the organiser's time are different resources. An available room does not supply a facilitator, and eight hours available next month cannot meet a promise due tomorrow. Preserve the capability and timing that constrain the work.

Use [MA.1 (MA.1:4)](../ma/08-ma-1---build-the-resource-consumption-and-cost-model.md#ma14---solution) to obtain a missing resource-consumption and cost account. It connects outputs, preparation, attempts and rework to resources and to the applicable payments. Use [MA.2 (MA.2:4)](../ma/09-ma-2---explain-the-cost-and-use-of-capacity.md#ma24---solution) when supplied, used, reserved and unavailable capacity have been confused. Keep donated time visible as a constrained contribution. Express its monetary value only when the receiving comparison needs and can justify that valuation.

Recover what is already committed elsewhere. [OCE.11 (OCE.11:4)](../oce/33-oce-11---coordinate-organization-change-work-with-continuing.md#oce114---solution) helps arrange overlap with continuing work; [OPS.5 (OPS.5:4)](../ops/33-ops-5---admit-work-and-limit-starts.md#ops54---solution) supplies admission against the provider's actual commitments. Obtain those results from the people who can change the work or its provision. A community schedule by itself cannot release a contributor from their other duties.

Use enough detail to decide. A small group may need only the next session's tasks and contributors. A programme with staff, several locations or uncertain demand may need alternative schedules and a fuller account. Deepen a quantity when its plausible range changes the promise or the support decision.

#### CB.9:4.3 - Compare ways to provide the contribution

Construct support alternatives from the actual gap. Members may share a task, an employer may provide time, a sponsor may provide space, or a qualified supplier may perform paid work. A smaller or less frequent activity can remove a demand; changing a payment source alone cannot.

For each feasible alternative, compare the received contribution, the burden on each party and the consequential conditions. Ask who bears preparation and interruption, who controls the resource, which uses are allowed and what happens at renewal. A free platform may cost migration work or impose limits that matter more than its current price. Obtain its applicable terms when choosing it.

Separate the resource comparison from the funding mechanism. Membership dues, voluntary contributions, grants, paid services and commercial sponsorship can fund similar work while changing who can enter and whose interests receive attention. Explain these effects to affected participants. A group can choose mutual provision without becoming a business, and a commercial programme can pay for work while retaining a voluntary discussion space.

Use [OPS.14 (OPS.14:4)](../ops/47-ops-14---relate-throughput-cash-and-operating-consequences.md#ops144---solution) for an unresolved operating and financial comparison. It follows the alternatives through actual resource use, displaced work, payment timing and funding. MA supplies the cost relations. A favourable total at the end of a quarter does not pay an earlier room deposit.

Choose with the authorised parties. State the accepted loss: fewer sessions, paid coordination, a narrower response promise or less sponsor reporting, for example. Keep the alternative or missing decision that would reopen the choice. Participation in an activity alone does not authorise the organiser to accept a new obligation on members' behalf.

#### CB.9:4.4 - Agree contributions, benefits and control

Make each consequential commitment understandable to its contributor and receiver. State the work or resource, period, limits, support, compensation or reimbursement where applicable, and how an absence or withdrawal will be handled. Obtain agreement from the person or owner who can actually provide it.

Distinguish an invitation to contribute from an accepted assignment. Ask what a volunteer is willing and able to do; leave a reachable way to decline, reduce or end the contribution. If continuity requires fixed delivery, provide an arrangement that can carry that obligation, such as paid work, a confirmed rota with backup or a smaller promise. Appreciation through CB.6 can support a relationship; it does not supply missing labour.

Agree how benefits and costs are shared. Explain who can use the result, whether someone pays for privileged access, what a sponsor receives and how contributors are credited. Bring a contested distribution to those with the relevant mandate. Agreement can include an accepted asymmetry, such as an employer paying while independent practitioners receive open guidance; equal contributions are not a prerequisite.

When use and renewal of a shared resource are the difficulty, use [ECO.7 (ECO.7:4)](../../eco/17-eco-7---coordinate-use-and-renewal-of-a-shared-resource.md#eco74---solution). Its arrangement connects access, use, upkeep, observation, response and adaptation. CB.13 supplies a missing community mandate; resource owners retain their actual rights. A majority vote among users cannot grant access that the owner has not provided.

Make sponsor influence legible. Agree who selects activities, who controls contributed material and what information may be returned. Provide a way to raise a conflict without risking loss of ordinary participation. If a proposed condition changes the original offer, return it to affected people before relying on their earlier agreement.

#### CB.9:4.5 - Make the arrangement operable and try a shortfall

Obtain the allocations, access and practical support needed for the next activity. OCE.6 helps establish assignments and enabling relations; the receiving provider must accept work that depends on its resources. Check that a person can perform their part, rather than treating a named owner as provision.

Run one ordinary occurrence through the arrangement. Can the host enter the venue, can the facilitator prepare, and can the promised result reach its user? Inspect the first use and obtain contributors' accounts of work that the plan omitted. Correct the promise or the provision where they differ.

Also examine the shortfall most likely to defeat continuation: a key person's absence, a delayed payment, a loss of access or demand above the agreed limit. Decide who will reduce, postpone or cancel the affected promise and how participants will learn that decision. A simple backup may be enough. If no backup is feasible, make the limit part of the offer.

Keep the response proportionate to the reliance. A casual discussion can be postponed; a promised professional service may need a qualified replacement and explicit settlement with its users. Ask the relevant owner for the needed arrangement before accepting that reliance.

#### CB.9:4.6 - Reconcile support with actual work

At a useful interval, compare the accepted promise, delivered result, resource use and remaining support. Ask contributors privately where public gratitude could make a burden difficult to report. Look for extra preparation, repeated requests outside the agreed scope, deferred rest and work silently displaced elsewhere.

Distinguish a shortfall in supply from a change in the contribution itself. More applicants may require admission or a smaller promise. A sponsor's new sales objective may require a new agreement. An activity with little practical value needs CB.10 and possibly CB.1; simply funding more of it may preserve the wrong offer.

Revise the affected commitment with its holder and receiver. Obtain the replacement allocation, payment or reduced expectation and communicate it to those who rely on the old one. Record only what people need to act and later settle the arrangement.

Before an important support period ends, establish whether renewal is real, conditional or absent. Use CB.11 for continuation, transfer, separation or ending that changes the arrangement as a whole. Preserve useful resources and settle current commitments even when the future programme is reduced.

### CB.9:5 - Archetypal Grounding

#### CB.9:5.1 - A funded room with an unfunded organiser

In a constructed repair community, two clinics each month require twelve expert-hours and six coordination hours. Two experts each offer six hours, but the organiser offers only four. Room and consumable payments total 120 currency units per month. Paying those 120 units leaves the two-hour coordination gap.

The group compares two supported options for the next three months. One monthly clinic requires six expert-hours, three coordination hours and 60 units. An available unrestricted grant can fund it. Keeping two clinics requires the original 120 units plus two paid coordination hours at 30 units each, or 180 units per month. A sponsor offers 540 units for the period with recognition on the invitation and a report of the number of completed clinics.

The organiser verifies that the additional coordinator can handle booking confirmations within the paid two hours, that the experts still agree to their six hours, and that the sponsor pays before bookings fall due. Members on alternating work schedules can reach only one of the two dates. The community's authorised decision therefore keeps both clinics, accepting the additional payment and sponsor recognition to preserve that access. The coordinator accepts the work and receives the booking access; the sponsor receives the agreed aggregate report.

The first clinic reveals another hour spent returning tools and resolving an unfinished booking. The group places that work with a willing contributor who has access and time. It also narrows the invitation: specialist advice is available at the clinics, with no promise of continuous replies between them.

When one expert becomes unavailable for a later date, the other cannot safely cover both tables. The organiser uses the agreed response: reduce that clinic to one table and tell booked participants what can be served.

#### CB.9:5.2 - When a voluntary contribution becomes scheduled production

A professional association invites members to contribute examples when they wish. A new customer contract would require revised examples every Friday in a prescribed format. Before promising that service, the association separates the open contribution space from the scheduled production.

A willing qualified member accepts paid editorial work with allocated time and a replacement arrangement. Others can continue offering examples under the original voluntary terms. The customer receives only the production promise that has been provided. If the paid role cannot be filled, the association defers that contract instead of converting the contributors' earlier enthusiasm into a weekly duty.

#### CB.9:5.3 - Keeping access without compulsory payment

A neighbourhood group wants newcomers with little money to use a shared room. Some members can fund bookings; others offer preparation and cleanup. The group obtains the owner's permission, compares the upkeep burden and agrees supported open sessions under an existing mandate.

At the first session, the paid booking and cleanup both occur. Later, an additional session has no willing cleanup contributor. The group postpones it rather than automatically charging the newcomers or leaving the task to the usual host. If the same conflict persists, ECO.7 helps revise access and upkeep together.

#### CB.9:5.4 - Independent users and sufficient existing provision

A small public guide has a willing maintainer, authorised hosting and a clear way to report corrections. Its users apply the practice independently. When the next update fits that established provision, the maintainer makes it under the existing arrangement. A future loss of hosting or maintenance would reopen the provision question.

### CB.9:6 - Bias-Annotation

The organiser may underestimate their own time, while a sponsor can make its visible benefits easier to report than participants' quiet gains. People with less money or less control over their work time can be excluded by an apparently equal contribution rule. Ask whose burden is missing from the arrangement and whether they can decline without losing unrelated access. A favourable account from continuing contributors leaves departed or silent people unexamined.

### CB.9:7 - Conformance Checklist

For an arrangement on which people will rely, establish the following at the detail needed for that reliance:

- The promised result and support period are understandable to the receiving parties.
- Material labour, capability, access and payment requirements have willing, authorised provision.
- Other commitments and the timing of payments have been considered where they affect feasibility.
- Contribution terms, benefits and sponsor influence are understandable to the affected people.
- Volunteers can decline or revise their contribution, and any dependable service has appropriate provision.
- An ordinary use and a consequential shortfall have a workable response.
- Actual burden and the end of the support period can change the promise before it becomes unsupported.

The arrangement can support reliance when the contributions, permissions and terms actually hold. Its description makes those conditions inspectable. Delivery and later value require their own observations.

### CB.9:8 - Common Anti-Patterns and How to Avoid Them

| Failure | Better move |
| --- | --- |
| Count a grant as complete provision | Recover the qualified work, access and timing that the money still needs to obtain. |
| Treat donated time as unlimited | Agree a contribution and its limit with the person who will perform it. |
| Promise every department a different benefit from one organiser | Reconcile the receiving results and provide the work each requires. |
| Use gratitude to carry a continuing service deficit | Provide the missing work, reduce the service or end the unsupported promise. |
| Count an allocated cost reduction as spendable cash | Obtain the actual capacity and payment consequences through the applicable MA and OPS methods. |
| Accept a sponsor's new condition under members' old agreement | Return the changed offer and obtain the affected decisions. |
| Keep a schedule by silently shifting the burden to the remaining volunteer | Apply the agreed reduction, replacement or postponement and revise the arrangement. |

### CB.9:9 - Consequences

Participants receive a more credible offer, contributors can see and question their obligations, and supporters can understand what their provision enables. A small activity can continue with mutual support; a service that requires dependable production can receive a different arrangement.

Making hidden work visible can reduce the apparent scale of the programme or increase its stated cost. Obtaining agreement and testing a shortfall also take effort. The gain is a choice made while alternatives remain available, rather than an unexplained cancellation or an exhausted organiser.

### CB.9:10 - Rationale

A community promise is realised through several people's work and through resources they may not control in common. Connecting those contributions makes a support decision actionable. Separating the benefit, contribution and authority of each party also exposes conflicts that a single budget cannot resolve.

Voluntary and paid contributions can coexist when their terms are clear and workable. The decisive question is what reliance the arrangement can support. Reopening that question when work or conditions change protects both the useful contribution and people's ability to choose their participation.

### CB.9:11 - SoTA-Echoing

The method compares ways to support the same community activity or programme period, including the effort of arranging that support. Use operating and accounting methods when unresolved resource, capacity or payment questions can change the choice.

| David Spinks, *The Business of Belonging* (2021), physical PDF pp.59, 62–76 and 191–197 | Adapt the connection between objectives, programme work, support and organiser effort. | MA.1, MA.2, OPS.5 and OPS.14 | Adopt actual resource dependencies, available capacity, commitment admission and incremental payment timing when these questions are unresolved. | This adds calculation where it can change the choice; a small already supported activity can use a simpler account. The calculation does not grant authority. |
| OCE.6, OCE.11 and ECO.7 | Adopt actual assignments and enabling relations, compatibility with continuing work, and shared-resource access/upkeep arrangements. | These suppliers develop the organisational and economic work; CB.9 connects it to the participants' offer and the supporters' terms. A common purpose alone supplies none of those permissions. |

The method combines a community contribution with effective provision and revisable terms. It retains independent access and non-commercial support as positive options. Current provider terms, payment conditions and applicable rules must be obtained for the particular arrangement; a historical example cannot establish them.

### CB.9:12 - Relations

- **CB.1** supplies the worthwhile contribution; **CB.2–CB.5** supply the participation and work that consume resources.
- **CB.6 and CB.7** support recognition, willing organisers and succession. Use CB.9 to obtain the needed work under agreed terms.
- **CB.8 and CB.13** supply workable expectations, complaints and community decision authority.
- **CB.10** investigates value and burden; **CB.11** changes or ends an arrangement when support or purpose changes.
- **MA.1 and MA.2**, **OPS.5 and OPS.14**, **OCE.6 and OCE.11**, and **ECO.7** supply the dependent cost, capacity, assignment and shared-resource results described in the Solution.
- **CB.14** can require a separately provided development project. Independent carriers can use the resulting practice without joining the funding or discussion group.
