## EXD.Reference:3 - Obtain and Carry the Subject Explanation

Recover the account that supports the connection the recipient needs. It may be a mathematical definition and derivation, a causal finding, a Method account or a recommendation's rationale. Select its relevant claims while preserving answer-changing conditions, alternatives and limits. The minimum useful account can be very short.

For a nonempty finite list, its mean is the sum divided by the count. Group A contains 2 and 4, with sum 6 and mean 3. Group B contains six 8s, with sum 48 and mean 8. Equal weight per individual gives `(6 + 48)/8 = 6.75`. Equal weight per group gives `(3 + 8)/2 = 5.5`. The intended weighting determines which result answers the question.

If a consequential connection lacks grounds, identify the missing premise, derivation or other subject contribution. A causal explanation requires suitable evidence about the cause. A recommendation needs the premises and conditions supporting it, including any alternatives material to its rationale. A mathematical step may follow from a definition or a derivation already available. Use a qualified existing result when it supplies the connection. Otherwise state the conditional answer or the limit and seek further subject work when the task warrants it.

Start with the work the recipient was asked to do. A story can show how a procedure ran after someone chose its inputs, assigned responsibilities and settled exceptions. Which of those choices will the recipient have to make? Use the subject practice to explain how to make each consequential choice, which facts bear on it and how to judge the result. Give the reader enough to use that reasoning beyond the episode when the assignment calls for it. The story itself can carry the whole method if the reader can recover the choices, their grounds and their range from it. Use C.39.WA when the account of the whole still needs to be built.

When revising a section, keep the inference or design choice the reader came to learn in view. An added case can teach a different decision even when both decisions belong to the same discipline and level of model abstraction. Use [EXD.6:4.2](14-exd-6---compare-explanations-for-the-recipient-s-use-and-dec.md#exd642---identify-the-smallest-material-change) to decide where that case belongs.

With the requested work in view, follow the explanation’s conclusion back to its premises and operations and then forward from them to the answer. At the first consequential gap, identify whether an input is absent, an operation unexplained, an inference unsupported or the connection to the question missing. Select any needed subject work accordingly. A change of expression alone cannot establish a missing premise.

Use F.19 when the gap concerns the assertion, referent or instruction conveyed by a bounded expression. Identify what cannot be recovered and which inference or action this prevents. If a premise, derivation or operation is missing from the account, obtain it through the appropriate subject Method, following the explanation backward and forward as above. Return the supported assertion or operation, its grounds and unresolved conditions to the passage, then use F.19 to check the revised expression.

For an account of a Method, use A.3.2.DE:4.1 to recover who or what acts on which input, what the operation yields and why it is justified. Use C.39:4.2–4.5 to develop a needed operation or connection, working back from the receiving result and forward through the proposed steps. For a language of Methods, E.8.ML:4.1–4.4 explains how independently useful results fit together and why the Methods have their stated boundaries. Carry the applicable contribution and its conditions into the explanation.

When an example is a model, identify both the model the author supplies and the model the reader needs to construct or use. When the case illustrates a method of explanation, identify what the case models and which choice the explainer demonstrates by using it. To explain a transdisciplinary principle (L3), state the relation and the conclusion it supports. To construct a disciplinary interpretation (L2), identify what plays each part of the relation in that discipline, what warrants the correspondence, and which conclusion follows. Give the conditions under which that conclusion holds. A known limit or an unresolved correspondence matters when the reader needs to work beyond them. Two accounts from different disciplines can each describe just one event.

A disciplinary method can be illustrated by a model of a recurring procedure at one site (L1) or by a model identifying one particular occurrence (L0). A reader may recover the disciplinary rule from the latter, or learn only how that occurrence unfolded. Performing the work and modeling it are different activities. Identify what the reader actually produced. B.5.MR:4.3 names the levels of model abstraction and refinement. For a claim of strict refinement, use B.5.MR:4.2. Show that the proposed model has at least one feasible completion and that every completion it admits, read under the agreed interpretation, satisfies the assertions of the earlier model. Identify an answer the earlier model admits that none of these completions can give.

To design an example that distinguishes two rules, choose a question on which they give different answers under attainable conditions. Establish the interpretation from the subject conditions independently of the answer being tested. If the rules give the same answer to the selected question, the example illustrates that answer. The grounds for it can still differ. A single observation that both rules allow can leave open which other actions each permits. For probabilistic predictions, consider how likely each rule makes the observed result. EXD.2:4.1–4.3 shows how to choose a condition that distinguishes them.

Consider a principle about simultaneous use of a resource. If the users’ combined demand exceeds the capacity available in the same interval, they cannot all receive the service they require in that interval. In a rehearsal-booking model, each exclusive booking occupies the entire room. The capacity rule and a rule that every shared facility must serve one user at a time both rule out overlapping bookings. In a computing model, a job can require one or two cores for the interval. On a server with two independently assignable cores, two jobs needing one core each fit. The rule requiring every user to wait their turn excludes that assignment. If each job needs both cores, their demands exceed capacity. This settles what the cores permit. Other resources or scheduling rules may impose further limits.

Use the method appropriate to a gap in the subject account. RMP.2:4.4 helps construct an investigation that could distinguish proposed answers. PSDA.14:4.2–4.4 helps justify a recommendation from its premises, alternatives and comparison, and PSDA.19 addresses conditions of the advising engagement. When a person needs to learn the relation, HCD.6, HCD.9 and HCD.10 guide representative tasks, feedback and assistance. Use A.6.3.NAR when a narrative expression helps connect supplied source relations.

For a research question, a puzzling observation may lead to competing explanations, criticism of those explanations against the available evidence before an experiment, and an initial report of what can already be established. An adequate source may already answer the question. If it remains open, weigh the evidence an attainable study could provide against the burden of obtaining it. Use RMP to qualify the question, select a strategy or a justified stopping point and revise the claims affected by the results.

Use the warranted subject account as the basis for the explanation. The six methods below address questions about the recipient, example, expression, dialogue, learning and comparison. Enter where the remaining task requires a contribution.
